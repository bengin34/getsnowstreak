Beginner first ski trip: how to prepare, what to bring

Prepare for a first ski trip with four to six weeks of squats, balance and easy cardio, rent well-fitted boots and a helmet, and keep day one short. The first day is the riskiest: one study found first-day skiers were injured at about 2.2 times the expected rate.

Updated: 2026-11-04

A first ski trip has a lot going on: boots that feel like ski buckets on concrete, a lift you have never ridden and a group that expects you to enjoy it. Most of what goes wrong is predictable and avoidable with a few weeks of preparation and some sensible choices on day one. This guide covers what to do before you leave, what to bring, how to spend the first three days, and what the injury research says about beginners.

How risky is a first ski day?

Skiing is not the most dangerous sport, but the first day is the riskiest day. A case-control study at Scotland's three largest ski areas over three seasons found that first-day participants made up injured cases at about 2.2 times the rate expected. Independent risk factors among them were age under 17 or over 26, snowboarding, taking professional lessons and using rented or borrowed equipment[1]. The authors recommended education on equipment, protection and staying within your ability.

Two points need care. First, the lesson finding does not mean you should skip lessons. The authors could not explain it, and a likely reading is that beginners who take lessons are more ambitious, ski more hours or end up on harder terrain sooner. Second, those numbers come from one region and one period. Still, the pattern is consistent with other work: a 2026 review of studies covering about 408 million amateur skier-days found falls to be the leading cause of injury and limbs the most commonly hurt[2], and a hospital series from China found beginners were far more likely to have lower-leg injuries than upper-limb ones (odds ratio 4.58)[3]. Beginners fall, and their legs take it.

The conclusion is not to be afraid. It is to make your legs and your plan ready for falls, fatigue and slow progress.

Prepare your body four to six weeks before

You do not need to be an athlete. You need legs that can hold a bent-knee stance for a few minutes at a time and balance on one foot. If you do nothing else, do these three things for four to six weeks:

  • Squats and wall sits. Start with 20 to 30 second holds and add a few seconds each week. The position is the same one you will use on skis.
  • Single-leg balance. 30 seconds per leg, more on a folded towel. See ski balance exercises.
  • Some cardio. Walking briskly, cycling or stairs for 20 to 30 minutes, three times a week. Ski days are long, and lift queues do not rest you.

The complete plan is in the ski fitness plan, and ski training for beginners goes through the basics step by step. If you only have a week, read ski exercises at home and pick five moves.

Gear: what to rent and what to bring

Renting is normal for a first trip. What matters is the fit and setup:

  • Boots. They should be snug with no heel lift, and your toes should touch the front only when you stand straight. Tell the technician you are a beginner. Painful boots end your day by noon.
  • Bindings. The shop sets release values from your weight, height, age and ability. Give honest information, and do not ask for a higher setting to feel safer.
  • Helmet. Most rental shops offer them. Wear one.
  • Clothing. Three layers: a thin base, a warm mid layer, a waterproof shell. Cotton gets wet and stays cold.
  • Small things. Gloves or mittens (bring a spare pair), goggles for flat light, sun protection on your face, a small bottle of water, a lip balm.

A sensible three-day plan

Day 1: ski school, short. Book a group lesson or a private hour at the start. Learn to walk in boots, to stop, to get up after a fall and to use the beginner lift. Stop after three to four hours even if you feel great; tired beginners fall more. The research above on first days is a reason for a short day one[1], and in a survey of 588 skiers with ACL injuries about a third were hurt on their first day on snow[4].

Day 2: repeat and extend. Practise on the same easy slope, then try one slightly longer green run. Your legs will be sore: that is normal. Read what helps with sore legs after skiing for the evening routine, and consider a lesson again.

Day 3: link turns. By now your body has adapted a little. Link easy turns, stop at the side of the piste and look uphill before you start again. Do not follow friends onto red runs before you can stop reliably on a green one.

For ideas on how many days make sense over a whole winter, see how many ski days per season.

Slope manners that keep you safe

  • Stop only at the edge or where you can be seen from above, never just below a roll.
  • The skier in front has the right of way. Look uphill before you start.
  • Keep pace with your group's slowest member, not the fastest.
  • Pick runs by the colour and difficulty, not by who you follow.
  • Drink water. Cold air and altitude dry you out without making you feel thirsty.

When to stop and when to see a doctor

Stop for the day if your legs shake, if you are cold to the point of shivering or if you start making the same mistake over and over. After a fall, see the ski patrol or a doctor if you have sharp pain, a joint that swells or feels unstable, a head blow, or numbness. Do not try to ski off an injury. Knee injuries are common in skiers, and an early diagnosis helps recovery.

Questions

How long should I prepare for my first ski trip?

Four to six weeks of three short sessions a week is a good target. Even two weeks of squats, balance and walking helps.

Should a beginner take a ski lesson?

Yes. Learning to stop and fall safely is worth more than any exercise. Keep the first day short whether or not you have a lesson.

Should I rent or buy my gear?

Rent for the first trip. Make sure boots fit well and the shop sets bindings to your real weight, height and ability.

What fitness do I need to start skiing?

Legs that can hold a bent-knee stance, some single-leg balance and basic cardio. You do not need to be an athlete.

Why are my legs so sore after the first day?

Skiing makes your thighs brake under load, which causes soreness for a day or two. It is common and usually fades without treatment.

When should I see a doctor after a fall?

With sharp pain, swelling, a joint that feels unstable, numbness or any blow to the head, get checked before skiing again.
