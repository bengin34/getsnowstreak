Snowboard workout: train for edges, landings and falls

A good snowboard workout trains what the board asks of you: a long half-squat on two edges, rotation through your core, soft landings, and arms strong enough to get you up off the snow. Below is a 20-minute bodyweight routine for home, plus what the research says about wrist injuries and wrist guards.

Updated: 2026-11-02

What does snowboarding demand that skiing doesn't?

On skis, each leg works on its own. On a snowboard, both feet are strapped sideways onto one board, so you can't step out of trouble. Everything runs through two edges. On the toe edge you press through the balls of your feet and lean slightly forward. On the heel edge you sit back into your heels with your shins against the highbacks. Turning means switching between them, again and again, in a half-squat that never fully lets go.

Three more things set riders apart:

  • The stance is asymmetric. Regular or goofy, your front leg steers and your back leg pushes and balances. After a few seasons, one side is often stronger and one hip tighter.
  • You get up from the snow a lot. Every stop on a flat cat track, every strap-in at the top of the lift, every fall ends with you pushing yourself up with both feet still attached. That is a squat from the ground, or a push-up and a roll, dozens of times a day.
  • Falls go onto your arms. A long-running study at a US ski resort found that wrist injuries were the most common snowboarding injury: 27.6% of all snowboard injuries, against 2.8% in skiing. Riders also had more shoulder, collarbone and ankle injuries, while skiers had far more knee ligament injuries[1]. Part of the reason is simple: with both feet fixed to the board, you break a fall with your arms[2].

So a snowboard workout shares the leg base of a ski workout, then adds more side-to-side work, more rotation, more upper body and a plan for falling.

Which muscles matter most for snowboarding?

Your legs carry the load. Quads and glutes hold the flexed riding stance for minutes at a time and take every bump and landing. Calves and the muscles along your shins tip the board onto its edges: toes down for the toe edge, toes up for the heel edge. Your hips do more than you'd think. The outer hip muscles keep your knees from caving in when you lean the board over.

Your core links your upper body to the board. Turns start with your shoulders and hips rotating where you want to go, and your trunk has to stay firm when the board chatters over ice. Then the upper body: shoulders, triceps and wrists push you up off the snow and take the falls. Most ski programs skip that part. Riders shouldn't.

A bodyweight snowboard workout you can do at home

No gym needed. You need floor space, a wall and about 20 minutes. Run it as a circuit: 40 seconds of work, 20 seconds of rest, 3 rounds. If you're new to training, start with 30 seconds on and 30 off. Warm up first for two minutes: march in place for a minute, then swing each leg forward and back.

  1. Stance squat. Feet at binding width, a bit wider than your shoulders, toes turned out slightly. Sit back and down until your thighs are near parallel, chest up, knees over your toes. Stand up through your whole foot.
  2. Toe-heel rocks. Stay in a quarter squat. Rock onto the balls of your feet and hold for two seconds, then back onto your heels with your toes lifting and hold again. That's your toe edge and heel edge, without the board.
  3. Lateral lunge. Step wide to one side, push your hips back and keep the other leg straight. Push back to the middle. It trains the hips and inner thighs that steady you on an edge.
  4. Single-leg deadlift. Stand on one leg, hinge at the hip and reach toward the floor while the free leg goes back. Keep your hips level. Do both sides and start with the weaker one.
  5. Wall sit in stance. Back against the wall, knees bent, feet at binding width. This is the burn of a long run, under control. Shift your weight to your front foot for 10 seconds, then to your back foot.
  6. Get-ups. Sit on the floor with knees bent and heels close to your hips, as if strapped in. Lean forward, reach your arms ahead and stand up. Sit back down slowly. Use one hand on the floor if you need it.
  7. Push-ups. Hands under your shoulders, body in one line. Put your knees down if full push-ups are too hard. These are the arms you push off the snow with.

The asymmetric stance is easy to forget at home. Do every single-leg move on both sides, and now and then set up your stance squats and wall sits with your other foot forward, like riding switch. How do you train your core for rotation?

Riders need two kinds of core strength: turning the upper body on purpose, and staying firm when the board gets knocked around. Three moves cover both:

  • Side plank. Elbow under your shoulder, feet stacked or staggered, hips high. Hold 20 to 40 seconds per side. It trains the side of your trunk that holds you over each edge.
  • Plank with shoulder taps. High plank, feet wide. Tap one shoulder with the opposite hand without letting your hips rock. Slow is harder than fast.
  • Standing rotation. In your riding stance, arms out in front, turn your shoulders toward the nose of an imaginary board, then toward the tail, with your hips quiet. That's the separation you use to start a turn. Keep it controlled. This is about range and timing, not speed.

How do you land jumps without hurting your knees?

Every landing, from a roller on the side of the run to a kicker in the park, is a load your legs have to absorb. Land stiff and the ground pushes back harder. In a lab study, landings with deeper knee bend produced lower ground reaction forces, and the hip and knee muscles absorbed more of the energy[3]. Parks are also where many riders get hurt: in the US resort data, 21.8% of snowboard injuries happened in terrain parks[1].

Train landings before the season:

  • Squat jump, stance landing. Jump straight up from a stance squat. Land on the balls of your feet first, then let ankles, knees and hips bend together. Freeze for two seconds. A quiet landing is a good landing.
  • Lateral line hops. Hop side to side over a line on the floor, feet together, small and quick. This builds the springiness you need in choppy snow.
  • Knees over toes. On every landing your knees point the same way as your toes. If they cave inward, make the jump smaller.

Riders get fewer knee ligament injuries than skiers[1], but knees still take a beating from landings and caught edges. The knee injury prevention guide covers balance and knee control in more detail.

How should you fall on a snowboard?

You will fall. The goal is to fall in a way your wrists survive. Most snowboard instructors teach the same basics:

  • Toe-side fall (forward): drop onto your knees first, then onto your forearms. Don't shoot your arms out straight.
  • Heel-side fall (backward): sit down toward the snow and roll onto your back, chin tucked. Don't reach back with your hands.
  • Make fists. Open hands on straight arms put the whole impact on your wrists.

Strength helps here too. Push-ups and planks prepare your shoulders and arms, and get-ups train the move you'll repeat all day.

Do wrist guards really help snowboarders?

Most of the evidence says yes. In a randomized trial with 5,029 snowboarders, the group that wore wrist protectors had 8 wrist injuries and the group without had 29[4]. A systematic review concluded that wrist guards may protect against wrist injuries, but found no consensus on which design works best[5]. A worry was that guards might shift fractures higher up the arm. A 40-year study of skiers and snowboarders found that wearing them was not linked to more forearm fractures[2].

In practice: wear them every day you ride, especially in your first seasons and in the park. Pick a pair that fits snugly under or inside your gloves and still lets you do up your bindings.

How often should you train before the season?

Three sessions a week for six weeks is a solid target. Rotate a leg day, a balance and core day and a jump day, with a rest day between sessions. Short and regular beats one long session on Sunday. The six-week ski fitness plan lays out that structure, and it works the same whether you ride or ski.

If something hurts, rather than burns, stop that exercise. Pain in a wrist, shoulder or knee that lasts after training is a reason to see a doctor or physiotherapist, not to push through.

Questions

Is snowboarding harder on the body than skiing?

It loads it differently. Riders get more wrist, shoulder and collarbone injuries, while skiers get more knee ligament injuries. Both need strong legs.

What is the single best exercise for snowboarding?

A squat with your feet at binding width. It trains the riding stance directly. If you add a second one, make it the side plank.

Can I train for snowboarding at home without equipment?

Yes. Squats, lunges, single-leg deadlifts, wall sits, side planks, jumps and push-ups need only floor space and a wall.

Should beginners wear wrist guards?

Yes. Beginners fall the most, and a randomized trial found far fewer wrist injuries in snowboarders who wore wrist protectors.

How long before the season should I start a snowboard workout?

About six weeks, with three short sessions a week. If you have less time, start now. A few weeks still help.
