Legs burning when skiing: why it happens and what helps

Your thighs burn on skis because your quads brake through every turn while staying tense, which squeezes their own blood supply. Lactic acid is not the real cause. A more forward stance, shorter runs and a few weeks of wall sits, intervals and slow squats make the burn come later.

Updated: 2026-11-02

You know the moment. Halfway down a long red run your thighs start to burn, then shake, and you pull over to the side of the piste. It happens to good skiers too, especially on the first days of the season. The burn has clear causes, and most of them you can change, some on the slope today and the rest with a few weeks of training at home.

Why do your thighs burn when you ski?

Skiing asks your quads to do an unusual job. In most sports your thigh muscles push you up or forward. On skis they mainly brake. Every turn, your outside leg bends under load and the quads lengthen while they hold you. That is eccentric work. A study of elite giant slalom racers found that this braking phase lasted about twice as long as the push-off phase, and that muscle activity during it came close to the level of a maximal effort in the lab[1]. Your turns are slower and gentler, but the pattern is the same: your thighs brake far more than they push.

The second cause is the stance. In a flexed position your quads stay tense for the whole run, with hardly a moment to relax. Tense muscle squeezes its own blood vessels. Researchers measured the oxygen in the thigh muscle of 30 junior racers while they skied. In the lower giant slalom stance, blood volume in the muscle dropped more and oxygen levels fell further, reaching close to what you get when a cuff blocks blood flow to the leg. The authors called this consistent with compromised blood flow to the working muscle[2].

Put the two together and you get a muscle that works hard, works without pause and gets less fresh blood than it needs. That is a recipe for a burn that builds through every turn and fades within a minute or two of stopping.

Is it lactic acid?

Not in the way most people think. The old story says your muscles make lactic acid, the acid builds up and the acid burns. Exercise biochemists have taken that story apart. The acidity comes from other reactions in the working muscle, and producing lactate actually slows the drop in pH rather than causing it[3]. Lactate is a useful marker that your muscle is working hard. It is not the villain.

So what do you feel? In one experiment, researchers injected small amounts of the substances a working muscle produces into volunteers: acid (protons), lactate and ATP. Each one on its own caused little or no sensation. Together, at low levels, they caused a feeling of fatigue. At higher levels, they caused pain[4]. The burn is your nerves reading that mix as a warning, not a single acid eating your muscle.

This also explains why the burn goes away fast once you stop. The soreness you feel the next morning is a different thing. It comes from the braking work itself and builds over a day or two. If that is your problem, read what helps with sore legs after skiing.

Are you skiing in the backseat?

Ski instructors see one cause more than any other: the backseat. When your weight sits behind your feet, your hips drop back and your quads have to hold your body up like in a chair with no seat. You feel it early and hard.

Signs you are in the backseat:

  • You feel your heels and the back of the boot more than your shins and the tongue.
  • Your skis run away from you when the slope gets steeper.
  • Your hands drift behind your hips.
  • Your thighs burn on easy blue runs, not just on steep ones.

The fix is small. Bend your ankles so your shins press lightly into the boot tongues. Keep your hands in front, where you can see them. Stack your hips over your feet, not behind them. Standing taller with flexed ankles uses your skeleton to carry you, so your quads do less static holding. If you cannot feel the difference yourself, one lesson with an instructor is often worth more than any exercise.

How do you stop the burn on the slope?

You cannot train in the lift line, but you can ski smarter today.

  • Stop before it burns. Split a long run into two or three parts. A 30-second stop at the side of the piste lets blood back into your thighs. Waiting until your legs shake costs you more recovery time.
  • Relax between turns. Stand up a little as you move from one turn to the next. Even a brief release lets the muscle refill with blood.
  • Use the lift. Sit fully on the chair, let your legs hang and lift the skis now and then. That ride is your rest interval.
  • Warm up. Do two easy runs first. Your legs work better warm than cold on the first steep pitch.
  • Ski your level. Steep, icy or mogul runs keep you low and tense for longer. Save them for when your legs are fresh.
  • Take a real break. A lunch stop with food and water helps more in the afternoon than one more run on tired legs.

Which training stops your legs burning?

The best fix starts weeks before your first ski day. You want three things: muscles that can hold a position for a long time, muscles that recover fast between efforts, and muscles that are strong while lengthening. Bodyweight training at home covers all three.

Wall sits for holding power

A wall sit copies the static part of skiing. Slide down a wall until your knees are bent, hold, and breathe. Start with 30 seconds and add time each week. Keep your knees over your feet, not in front of your toes.

Intervals for the long run

A run is not one effort. It is a string of turns with tiny pauses. Interval training, with work and rest phases of 30 to 50 seconds, trains exactly that: work hard, recover a little, work again. Squats, skater hops and mountain climbers in short rounds build the endurance that keeps the burn away until the bottom of the slope.

Slow lowering for braking strength

Because skiing is mostly braking, train the braking. Take three seconds to lower into each squat or lunge, then stand up at normal speed. In a study with junior alpine skiers, six weeks of eccentric training three times a week increased lean thigh mass and jump height more than regular weight training alone[5]. You do not need their special bike. Slow squats and reverse lunges use the same principle.

Two to three sessions a week is enough to build muscle, according to the Swiss accident insurer Suva's ski fitness program[6]. Start six weeks out if you can. See when to start ski training and the full six-week ski fitness plan, or pick moves from ski exercises at home.

When is the burn not normal?

A muscle burn that fades when you stop is normal. Other things are not. See a doctor or physiotherapist if you feel sharp or stabbing pain, pain in the knee joint rather than the muscle, swelling, numbness, or pain that stays for days or gets worse. Stop any exercise that hurts, as Suva also advises[6]. Knee pain on skis deserves its own look: read how to protect your knees skiing.

Questions

Why do my thighs burn so fast when I ski?

Your quads brake through every turn and stay tense for the whole run, which limits their blood flow. Skiing in the backseat and untrained legs make it come sooner.

Is the burn caused by lactic acid?

No. Lactate production actually slows the drop in muscle pH. The burn comes from a mix of acid, lactate and ATP that your nerves read as a warning signal together.

How do I stop my legs burning on the slope?

Shift your weight forward with flexed ankles, stand up a little between turns, split long runs with short stops and rest your legs on the lift.

What exercises help with burning legs when skiing?

Wall sits for holding power, interval rounds of squats and hops for endurance, and slow lowering squats and lunges for braking strength. Two to three sessions a week for about six weeks.

Is it normal that my legs shake after a few runs?

On the first days of the season, yes. Take more breaks and ski easier runs. Sharp pain, knee joint pain or swelling is not normal: see a doctor or physiotherapist.
