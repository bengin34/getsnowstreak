Wall sit for skiing: how long to hold and how to progress

The wall sit is a good ski exercise for holding power in your thighs, because skiing keeps your quads tense in a bent-knee stance for a whole run. Start with 30 seconds, build toward 45 to 60 seconds with your thighs near parallel, and pair it with moving exercises, since a wall sit alone does not train braking through turns.

Updated: 2026-11-04

Search for ski fitness and the wall sit shows up almost every time. It is easy to explain, needs one wall and no equipment, and it feels like skiing: burning thighs, bent knees, a clock that moves too slowly. This guide covers what the wall sit really trains, how to do it well, a simple progression, and where it stops being useful.

Why the wall sit is linked to skiing

On skis you spend long stretches in a flexed stance with your quads tense and little chance to relax. That is mostly static or slowly changing effort, which is what the wall sit imitates. Researchers who measured the thigh muscle of 30 junior racers found that in the lower giant slalom stance, blood volume in the muscle fell and oxygen levels dropped more than in the higher slalom stance. The authors linked this to higher static load that may compromise blood flow to the working muscle[1]. A wall sit trains you to hold a loaded, bent-knee position without giving up. If you want the full story of why thighs burn on the slope, read legs burning when skiing.

What it does not do

Be honest about the limit. In giant slalom racers, the outside leg spent about twice as long lengthening under load as pushing off, and its muscle activity during that lengthening phase matched near-maximal isometric contractions[2]. Those are racers, and your turns are gentler, but the pattern holds: skiing is mostly slow, controlled lowering under load. A wall sit is isometric, so the muscle holds a fixed length. It builds the ability to stay in position, not the ability to absorb a bump or finish a turn. Treat it as one piece of a plan, next to squats and lunges that move.

How to do a wall sit properly

  1. Stand with your back flat on a wall and walk your feet forward until your knees sit above your ankles when you slide down.
  2. Slide down until your thighs are roughly parallel to the floor. Higher is fine at first.
  3. Keep your feet hip-width apart, knees pointing over your second toe, heels down.
  4. Rest your hands on your thighs or cross them over your chest. Pressing on your knees takes load away from the muscles you are training.
  5. Breathe normally. Holding your breath makes the hold feel harder than it is.

Foot placement matters less than you might expect, but small details do change which muscles work. In a 2022 study of 20 people, a wall squat with the ankle angled slightly toward plantarflexion produced more activity in the inner and front thigh muscles than a standard wall squat, while the outer thigh and hamstrings did not change significantly[3]. That is a small lab finding, not a rule, so there is no need to chase a perfect foot angle. Pick a position that feels stable and lets you keep your heels down.

How long should you hold?

There is no research-backed magic number for recreational skiers, so use the number that makes the last few seconds hard but clean. A practical range:

  • Weeks 1 to 2: 3 holds of 30 seconds, 30 seconds rest, thighs a little above parallel if needed.
  • Weeks 3 to 4: 3 holds of 35 to 45 seconds, thighs near parallel.
  • Weeks 5 to 6: 3 to 4 holds of 40 to 60 seconds, or add a short pulse up and down of a few centimetres at the end.

A whole run takes far longer than a minute, so do not chase a five-minute wall sit. Longer than about a minute, extra time mostly teaches you to tolerate boredom. Better progressions are lower depth, one leg at a time with the other foot lightly on the floor, or holding a light backpack. Stop a set when your form breaks, when your knees collapse inward, or when anything hurts in the joint rather than the muscle.

Where it fits in a week

The Swiss accident insurer Suva suggests training two to three times a week with a programme matched to your fitness level, and stopping any exercise that causes pain[4]. A wall sit fits easily as a finisher or one block inside a leg session. A simple week looks like this:

  • Session 1: squats, reverse lunges, wall sit, glute bridge, calf raise.
  • Session 2: balance and single-leg work.
  • Session 3: jumps and intervals, or a brisk walk or ride if you are new to jumping.

Common mistakes

  • Sitting too deep too early. If you cannot keep your heels down and knees tracking, go higher.
  • Feet too close to the wall. Knees push far past the toes and the exercise turns into a knee grind. Walk the feet out.
  • Using it as the whole plan. Skiing needs strength in motion, balance and some endurance as well.
  • Testing yourself every day. Timing max holds daily just tires you. Test once every two weeks.

Who should be careful

If you have knee pain, a past ligament injury or a diagnosed joint problem, ask a doctor or physiotherapist before you add holds to your routine. Sharp pain, swelling, or a knee that gives way is a reason to stop and get it looked at. Muscle burn is expected; joint pain is not. The programme from Suva is designed for healthy people and tells you to consult a doctor if you are unsure about your health[4].

Is the wall sit enough?

No, but it is a cheap and useful part. It builds tolerance for the held position you will be in on the slope, it is easy to measure, and it leaves your joints out of motion while you learn how a bent-knee stance should feel. Add moving leg work for the braking phase, balance work for the knees and some interval rounds for endurance, and you have something that matches what the sport asks from your legs.

Questions

How long should a wall sit be for skiing?

Start with 30 seconds and work toward 45 to 60 seconds with thighs near parallel, in 3 sets. Past a minute, make it harder with depth or one leg instead of adding time.

Is a wall sit better than squats for skiing?

Neither is better. A wall sit builds holding endurance in the bent-knee stance, while squats train moving under load. Skiing uses both, so do both.

How low should I go in a wall sit?

Aim for thighs near parallel with the floor, but only as low as you can keep heels down and knees tracking over your toes. Higher is fine for the first weeks.

How often should I do wall sits before ski season?

Two to three leg sessions a week for about six weeks, with a wall sit in each leg session. Rest a day between hard leg days.

Should I do wall sits if my knees hurt?

Muscle burn is normal, joint pain is not. If you have knee pain, swelling or a past injury, ask a doctor or physiotherapist before adding holds.
