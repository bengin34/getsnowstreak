Ski recovery between days: what helps and what is hype

Between ski days, the basics do most of the work: eat, drink, sleep and walk lightly. The evidence for active cool-downs, stretching and cold-water immersion is weaker than their reputation, so treat them as optional comfort, and cut the next day short if your legs are shaking.

Updated: 2026-11-04

You skied hard on Saturday, you want to ski again on Sunday, and your thighs have opinions. On a week-long trip that repeats five or six times. What you do in the evening can make the next morning easier, but a lot of popular recovery advice rests on thin evidence. This guide separates what is reasonably supported from what is mostly ritual, and gives you a routine that costs little.

What is actually going on in your legs

Most of the soreness after skiing is muscle soreness from braking work: your quads lengthen under load all day. It peaks one to two days after, so day two of a trip is often worse than day one. If you want the full explanation, read sore after skiing. Soreness is not the same as damage that needs rest; it is a normal response that fades and, importantly, fades faster the more regularly you do the activity.

That last part matters. A 2026 systematic review of delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) concluded that chronic training reduces it through repeated-bout adaptation: the same exercise causes less soreness next time[1]. For skiers, this means two things. Days three and four of a trip are usually easier than day two, and legs that were prepared before the trip start from a better place. See the ski fitness plan for that preparation.

Cool-downs

A 2018 narrative review compared active cool-downs (easy movement after exercise) with passive rest. The authors concluded that active cool-downs are largely ineffective for same-day and next-day performance, do not meaningfully reduce muscle soreness, and do not prevent injuries. They did speed the return of heart rate and breathing toward resting values and cleared blood lactate faster, which feels good but does not equal faster muscle repair[2].

Practical read: a ten-minute walk to the lift base or around the village is fine and pleasant. Do not expect it to fix tomorrow's legs.

Stretching

The Delphi consensus of 20 stretching researchers agreed that stretching improves range of motion and reduces stiffness, and also that it is not a complete injury-prevention strategy and does not acutely speed recovery[3]. If stretching your calves and hips in the evening feels good, do it. Keep it gentle and short, and do not push into pain trying to "flush out" soreness. It will not.

Cold water, hot tubs and gadgets

An umbrella review of 15 meta-analyses on cold-water immersion found reduced soreness up to 96 hours and lower creatine kinase (a muscle damage marker) at 24 to 72 hours, in protocols of 10 to 15 minutes at 10 to 15 degrees C. It also found no strength benefit, a possible blunting of strength gains with chronic use, and overall low to very low certainty[4]. For a ski trip where you are not trying to build strength that week, a cold plunge is a comfort choice, not a requirement. A hot tub is nicer and will not hurt, but the studies here do not tell us it speeds muscle recovery. Wear shoes on icy paths; people slip.

A simple evening routine

  1. Eat within a couple of hours. Carbohydrate and protein, a normal dinner is fine.
  2. Drink water. At altitude and after a day in dry cold air you lose more than you notice. Alcohol makes sleep worse, so keep it modest if you want to ski tomorrow.
  3. Walk ten minutes. Easy, flat, no hurry.
  4. Two or three gentle stretches for calves, quads and hips, 30 seconds each, if they feel good.
  5. Sleep. Aim for a normal night and plan a later first lift if you need it. I could not source trial data on sleep for ski trips specifically, but short nights make tired skiers, and tired skiers make mistakes.

How to ski the next day

  • Warm up on easy terrain. The first run should not be a black. A short routine before you click in is covered in ski warm-up before skiing.
  • Take your breaks early. Stop for a hot drink before you are empty, not after.
  • Lower the volume. If the first day was eight hours, make the second six. Your legs, not your ski pass, set the limit.
  • Stop when form drops. Legs shaking, turns getting sloppy, hands drifting behind you: those are signs to finish. You can read more in legs burning when skiing.

When to rest a whole day

Consider a rest day if walking downstairs hurts, your legs feel heavy rather than just tired, you are slow to warm up, or you have joint pain (knee, hip, back) as opposed to muscle soreness. A rest day on a long trip, ideally in the middle, is a reasonable plan; many people ski better on the days after it. If you have swelling, a joint that feels unstable, or pain that gets worse with movement, see a doctor or physiotherapist rather than skiing through it.

Food, water and altitude

Ski resorts sit high, the air is dry and cold, and you sweat under layers without noticing. Drink water through the day, not only in the evening. A big lunch of carbohydrate and some protein, plus a snack mid-afternoon, keeps energy from crashing late in the day, when tired legs and slow decisions tend to meet. Heavy alcohol the night before is a bad idea if you plan to ski hard: it disturbs sleep and leaves you slower and less coordinated in the morning.

If you wake with a headache, nausea or unusual breathlessness at altitude, take the day easy and see a doctor if it does not settle. Recovery is not only about legs.

What to do before the trip

The best recovery tool is arriving with strong legs. Eccentric strength is what skiing taxes, so slow squats, wall sits and step-downs are well spent time. If the trip is a few days away, the one-week plan is in ski fitness one week before your trip.

Questions

Does a cool-down help recovery after skiing?

A 2018 review found active cool-downs largely ineffective for next-day performance and soreness. A short easy walk is still fine for comfort, but it does not repair your muscles faster.

Should I stretch between ski days?

Gentle stretching can feel good, but expert consensus is that it does not acutely speed recovery. Keep it short and never push into pain.

Is a cold plunge worth it on a ski trip?

The evidence suggests it may reduce soreness, with low certainty and no strength benefit. It is optional. Skip it if it makes you miserable.

Why is day two of a ski trip often the worst?

Muscle soreness from braking work peaks around one to two days after. It usually eases by days three and four as your legs adapt.

When should I take a rest day from skiing?

If you have joint pain, heavy and slow legs, trouble with stairs, or any swelling, rest. See a professional if pain persists or a joint feels unstable.
