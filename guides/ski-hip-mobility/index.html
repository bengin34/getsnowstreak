Ski hip mobility: simple drills for a deeper, steadier stance

Skiing keeps your hips bent and working all day, so enough hip flexion and control helps you stay centered over your feet. Check your squat and hip range, then do a few minutes of hip openers and strength drills three times a week. Do not expect big changes in a few days.

Updated: 2026-11-04

Look at a good skier from the side: hips bent, chest up, weight balanced over the feet. That position is held for hours. It needs strong hips and enough range of motion to get into it without your back rounding or your knees collapsing. If your hips feel stiff in a deep squat, this guide is a practical place to start. It is not a cure for hip pain, and if you have pain you should see a clinician before stretching harder.

What "hip mobility" means for skiing

Three things count most:

  • Hip flexion: how far the thigh can come toward the chest. You need it to sit into a ski stance without rounding your back.
  • Rotation: turning the thigh in and out. Carving puts the legs in different positions, and the hip has to absorb the movement.
  • Hip control: glutes and the side-of-hip muscles holding the pelvis level while one ski carries most of the load.

The last one is strength, not flexibility, and it is often what people actually lack.

What the research says (and does not)

There are few studies of hip mobility in recreational skiers specifically. The closest practical evidence is from squatting. In a study of 101 healthy adults, hip flexion range of motion was associated with squat depth in men, together with bent-knee ankle dorsiflexion (R² = 0.435)[1]. Women's depth was associated instead with straight-knee dorsiflexion and dorsiflexor strength. So your ankles matter at least as much as your hips for a deep stance; see ski ankle mobility.

On stretching itself, an international Delphi panel of 20 experts agreed that stretching improves range of motion and reduces muscle stiffness, but is not a complete injury-prevention strategy and does not acutely speed recovery[2]. That is the honest frame: stretching can make a position easier to reach, but it is not magic armour.

A 2026 systematic review of 23 studies with 627 athletes looked at how long to stretch. Static stretching raised flexibility by about 2.4% against control, and stretches held longer than 60 seconds raised it more (6.2% pre to post) but cost some performance right afterwards (-1.8%). Fifteen of the 23 studies were low quality[3]. Practical read: keep holds around 30 seconds before activity, and save longer stretching for after, or for a separate session.

Check your hips in two minutes

  1. Deep squat hold. Feet shoulder width, toes slightly out, sit down as deep as you can with heels on the floor. Can you keep your chest up and knees over your toes? If your heels lift, check ankles. If your back rounds, hips are a candidate.
  2. Knee-to-chest. Lying on your back, pull one knee toward your chest while the other leg stays flat. The flat leg should not lift off the floor. If it does, the hip flexors on that side are tight.
  3. Single-leg stand. Stand on one leg for 30 seconds. Do your hips drop on one side or does your knee wobble inward? That points to weak hip stabilisers.

These are home checks, not clinical tests. Use them to see which side is weaker and to retest later.

Six drills

1. 90/90 hip switches

Sit with both knees bent at 90 degrees, one leg in front and one to the side. Rotate your knees to switch sides, keeping the chest tall. 10 switches. Go slowly and stay within a comfortable range.

2. Half-kneeling hip flexor stretch

One knee down, the other foot forward. Tuck the pelvis under and shift forward a little. Hold 30 seconds per side. If you feel it in the lower back, you are arching, not stretching.

3. Deep squat with a pause

Hold a doorframe or light weight for balance and sit into a deep squat for 5 seconds, pushing the knees out. 8 reps. See squats for skiing for form.

4. Cossack squat (side lunge)

Step wide, shift onto one leg while the other leg stays straight, then switch. It stretches the inner thigh and trains lateral control. 8 per side. Lateral lunges also show up in the balance and knee day of the ski fitness plan.

5. Glute bridge

Lie on your back, push the hips up, squeeze, lower slowly. 2 x 12. It builds the strength to hold the pelvis steady.

6. Side plank with leg lift

Hold a side plank and lift the top leg for 3 seconds. 3 reps per side. This is the control piece that stops hips dropping when you stand on one ski.

How often and for how long

Ten minutes, three times a week, is enough to notice a difference in four to six weeks. Warm up first (two minutes of marching and leg swings, as in ski warm-up before skiing). Mobility work after strength training is a comfortable pairing, because your muscles are warm and you are less tempted to push the stretch.

Do not chase pain. A mild pulling feeling is normal. Pinching in the front of the hip, a catching sensation, or pain that lingers the next day means ease off, and speak to a physiotherapist if it persists.

Where hip work fits in a ski plan

Hip drills are a supporting act. If you have six weeks, put strength, balance and cardio first, and use mobility as a short add-on at the start or end of a session. If you have one week, do the checks above, a few minutes of drills each day, and leave it there. Trying to gain range quickly with aggressive stretching usually just leaves you sore on the first ski day, which is the opposite of what you want.

Common mistakes

  • Stretching hard the night before a ski day. Muscles that feel overstretched are not helpful on edge.
  • Training flexibility only. If you can reach a deep position but cannot control it, strength drills matter more.
  • Ignoring ankles and boots.
  • Expecting results in a week.

Questions

What is hip mobility for skiing?

Enough hip flexion and rotation to hold a bent-hip stance, plus the strength in your glutes and side-of-hip muscles to keep the pelvis steady over one ski.

How do I know if my hips are tight?

Try a deep squat hold, a lying knee-to-chest, and a 30-second single-leg stand. Heels staying down, a flat resting leg and level hips are good signs. Failing one tells you where to focus, but it is not a diagnosis.

Should I stretch before skiing?

Short holds around 30 seconds after a warm-up are fine. Long holds past 60 seconds can reduce performance right afterwards in some studies, so keep those for after skiing or a separate session.

How long until my hips feel better?

Many people notice an easier deep squat after four to six weeks of regular work, but results vary and stop if you stop.

When should I see a clinician about my hips?

If you have pinching at the front of the hip, catching, pain that lingers, or pain after a fall, get it checked before stretching harder.
