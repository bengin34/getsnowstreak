Ski fitness plan: six weeks of pre-season training, no gym

A good pre-season ski plan is short and regular: three 17-22 minute bodyweight sessions a week for six weeks, rotating leg strength, balance and power. Start six weeks before your first ski day, add a little work time every two weeks, and don't stop completely once the season starts.

Updated: 2026-11-02

What does a ski fitness plan need to train?

Skiing asks your legs to hold a bent-knee stance for minutes at a time, absorb bumps and react when an edge catches. A review of alpine skiing physiology found that no single quality decides it. Technique matters most, but holding on to that technique over a long day or season depends on strength, endurance and power working together.[1] For a recreational skier that comes down to three jobs:

  • Leg strength for the thighs and glutes that hold your stance: squats, lunges, wall sits.
  • Balance and knee control so your knee stays over your foot when you are on one ski: single-leg work, lateral lunges.
  • Power and endurance for quick bursts and the last run before the lifts close: jumps, hops, mountain climbers.

You need no gym for any of it. Floor space and a wall are enough. Our guide to ski exercises at home shows every move with an easier and a harder version.

How many weeks and sessions do you need?

Six weeks at three sessions a week is a realistic target. The American College of Sports Medicine recommends two to three strength sessions a week for people new to resistance training, with small, steady increases once a workload feels easy.[2] Suva, the Swiss national accident insurer, gives the same frequency for its pre-season ski program with World Cup racer Loïc Meillard.[3]

Three sessions of about 20 minutes fit around work: Monday, Wednesday and Friday, or any three days with a rest day after each one. Count back from your first ski day. If you fly to Denver for a week in Breckenridge on December 20, start the week of November 8. Not sure how early is early enough? Read when to start training for ski season.

The six-week plan, week by week

Every session starts with a 2-minute warm-up: march in place for 60 seconds, then leg swings for 60 seconds. Then you work through five exercises as intervals: work, rest, next exercise. When all five are done, that is one round. Every two weeks the work gets longer and the rest shorter.

WeeksBeginnerAdvancedSession length
1-230 s work, 30 s rest, 3 rounds40 s work, 20 s rest, 3 rounds17 min
3-435 s work, 25 s rest, 3 rounds45 s work, 15 s rest, 3 rounds17 min
5-640 s work, 20 s rest, 4 rounds50 s work, 10 s rest, 4 rounds22 min

Each week you do all three sessions once, A, then B, then C. Over six weeks that is 18 sessions and a little under six hours in total.

Session A: leg strength

  • Squat: sit back as if onto a low chair, knees track over your toes.
  • Reverse lunge: step back, drop the back knee straight down, push up through the front heel.
  • Wall sit: back flat against the wall, thighs as close to level as you can hold. This is the closest thing to a long run you can do in your living room.
  • Glute bridge: feet flat, squeeze your glutes to lift your hips, pause at the top.
  • Calf raise: rise slowly, lower even slower.

Session B: balance and knees

  • Single-leg balance: stand on one leg, soft knee, eyes forward. Close your eyes once it gets easy.
  • Single-leg deadlift: hinge at the hip, free leg reaches back, standing knee stays over your foot.
  • Lateral lunge: big step to the side, sit into that hip, other leg straight.
  • Skater step: step diagonally back and across, like pushing off an edge, and hold for a beat.
  • Side plank: elbow under shoulder, one straight line from head to feet. Switch sides halfway.

Session C: power and endurance

  • Squat jump: squat, jump, land quietly on bent knees.
  • Skater hops: bound from side to side, land on one foot and stick it.
  • Lateral line hops: feet together, small quick hops over a line on the floor.
  • Mountain climbers: hands under shoulders, drive your knees in fast.
  • Plank: forearms down, ribs down, hips level.

Beginner or advanced: which level fits you?

Pick beginner if you don't train regularly or you are coming back after a break. Pick advanced if you already run, ride or lift two or three times a week. The ACSM rule applies here too: move up once the current level feels manageable, not before.[2] On the beginner level, the two jumping moves get swapped: a fast squat instead of the squat jump, and a skater step instead of skater hops. You can switch levels at any point.

A simple check: in the last round you should be working hard but still in control. If your knees cave in or your form falls apart, drop a level or take the longer rest.

Does pre-season training protect your knees?

Be honest about the evidence. A systematic review found almost no studies testing exercise programs for injury prevention in recreational skiers and snowboarders. Most published advice was about equipment.[4] In team sports, neuromuscular programs built on balance, landing and strength work cut the odds of an ACL injury by about half, with the clearest results in female athletes.[5] Session B uses the same kind of work. That makes it a reasonable bet, not a guarantee. More in our guide to ski knee injury prevention.

If a knee or your back hurts during an exercise, stop. See a doctor or physiotherapist before you carry on. Suva gives the same advice for its program.[3]

What if you have fewer than six weeks?

Start anyway. Begin at week 1, do the weeks you have and keep it at three sessions a week. Don't squeeze in extra sessions to catch up: arriving with tired legs costs you more on the mountain than one missed session. Even two or three weeks means your thighs have met a wall sit before they meet a long blue run. For the morning after day one, read sore legs after skiing.

How do you keep your fitness during the season?

Strength fades once you stop training, and the loss grows the longer the break lasts.[6] If you only ski a few weekends, keep one short session a week between trips instead of stopping completely. Session A or C works well for that. If your thighs still burn by lunchtime, our guide on why your legs burn when skiing explains what is going on.

Questions

How long should a pre-season ski workout be?

About 17 to 22 minutes, three times a week, is a realistic dose for recreational skiers. Short sessions you actually do beat long ones you skip.

Can I get fit for skiing without a gym?

Yes. Squats, lunges, wall sits, single-leg balance work, jumps and planks need only floor space and a wall.

How many weeks before ski season should I start training?

Six weeks before your first ski day is a good target. If you have less time, start now and do the weeks you have.

Should I train the day before a ski trip?

Keep the last two days before you ski easy. A short walk or light stretching is fine; hard leg work is not.

Is running or cycling enough to get ready for skiing?

Cardio builds endurance, but it does not train single-leg balance, the bent-knee hold or quick side-to-side moves. Add the strength and balance sessions.
