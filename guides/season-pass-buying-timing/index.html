Season Pass Buying Timing: When to Buy Your Ski Pass

Buy early if you are sure you will ski enough days and the pass has a refund or protection option you understand. Wait if your plans are open. Early windows often carry the best price, but that is a pattern to check with the resort, not a rule.

Updated: 2026-11-04

The short version

Season passes usually go on sale in spring, long before the first snow. Resorts and pass companies reward early commitment, and deadlines tend to move prices up over time. For the 2026-27 season, for example, Ikon passes went on sale on 12 March, and the press reported that Alterra and Vail announced their prices only days apart, with both raising prices from the year before[1]. I will not quote prices here. They change every year and differ by pass, resort and age group. What stays stable is the logic of timing, and that is what this page covers.

Why early buying is usually cheaper

A pass is a bet you place before the season. The seller gets money early and a guaranteed customer, so it offers the lowest price when the bet is hardest to make for you. Later in the year the same pass tends to cost more, or you pay the day-ticket price for each visit until you finally commit. Some products stop selling altogether: the Indy Pass FAQ for 2026-27 describes the pass as sold out, with a waitlist sale and a short public sale in September, and says new waitlists open only if it sells out again[2]. A pass that sells out is a different problem than a pass that gets pricier.

That said, the cheapest moment is not always the smartest. The question is whether the early price is lower than the cost you will really face once you know your plans.

What you give up by buying early

Three things, mostly.

  • Flexibility. In spring you do not know your work calendar, your budget in January, or whether your knee will be fine in February.
  • Information. Snow forecasts, resort changes and which resorts join a multi-resort pass can all change by autumn.
  • Cash. Some buyers prefer a payment plan. Check whether the resort offers one and what it costs you.

Refund and protection rules decide how much risk you carry. Read them before the price.

Read the refund and protection rules first

Rules differ a lot between passes, and they are the part people skip. A few examples from official pages, all for the 2026-27 season, so check the current terms:

  • The Ikon FAQ lists a refundable option with a deadline in January 2027. Whether you get a full refund, half or nothing depends on how many times your pass has been scanned[3].
  • The Indy Pass FAQ says all pass products are non-refundable except rollovers under pass protection. Protection has to be bought when you buy the pass, and you must have no redemptions that season to qualify[2].
  • Vail Resorts says Epic Coverage comes with most Epic passes at no extra charge. It covers events such as injury, sickness or job loss, and personal refund requests must be submitted within 30 days of the event. Weather closures and medical expenses are not covered[4].

Notice the pattern: protection is tied to timing and to how much you have already used the pass. "I will decide in February" can quietly void the safety net.

A simple way to decide

  1. Count your realistic days. Not your hoped-for days. Look at last season, subtract the trips that depended on luck, and be honest about weekends you actually left home.
  2. Find your break-even. Divide the pass cost by the day-ticket price you would otherwise pay. That number of days is the line. Our guide to whether a ski pass is worth it walks through it, and cost per ski day shows how to track it as you go.
  3. Check the downside rules. Is there a refund window, protection, a rollover, a payment plan? If the answer is no across the board, you carry the whole risk.
  4. Look at your calendar for the deadline. If the early window closes before you can know your plans, ask whether the saving beats the risk. A modest saving rarely justifies a pass you may only use three times.

If the break-even is well below the days you expect, buying early is usually a safe call. If it is close, waiting for more information, or choosing a smaller product, often wins.

When waiting makes sense

  • You are new to skiing and do not know whether you will like it. Rent and buy day tickets first. Our first ski trip guide helps with that.
  • Your trips are one or two weeks a year. Day or multi-day tickets often win at that volume.
  • Your health or knees are uncertain. A recovering injury and a pass with no protection is a poor combination.
  • You live far from the resort. Travel cost is part of your cost per day.

When buying early makes sense

  • You ski the same home mountain most weekends and know your pattern.
  • The pass includes a refund, protection or rollover that you have read and understood.
  • The product tends to sell out or close sales, as the Indy Pass FAQ describes for its own pass[2].
  • You already have your first ski day planned. Early buyers also get time to prepare. A ski fitness plan starting six weeks before opening day means you use those days well.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing the pass to nothing. Compare it to what you would really pay, which is often not the walk-up window price, but a multi-day or advance-purchase ticket.
  • Counting lift days, not skiing days. Half a day when your legs give out is not a full day. If your thighs fade early, see why your legs burn when skiing.
  • Forgetting add-ons. Blackout dates, partner resort limits, parking and food all change your real cost. Read the fine print for your pass.
  • Trusting last year's dates. Sale dates, deadlines and rules change every year. Check the resort or pass company's current page.

What I could not tell you

I cannot tell you the best day to buy. Nobody can, because the sale calendar and the price are set by each seller for each season. What you can do is learn when your resort usually announces, set a reminder for that window, and decide your break-even before the sale opens so the decision is calm instead of rushed.

Questions

When is the best time to buy a ski season pass?

Usually in the first sale window, often spring, if you are sure of your days and understand the refund or protection rules. The exact dates and prices are set by each resort, so check the current page.

Do season pass prices go up closer to winter?

Often they do, or the cheapest tier closes. Not every pass works that way, and some simply sell out, so check the seller's sale calendar for your pass.

Can I get a refund on a season pass?

It depends on the pass. Some offer refund windows tied to use, some sell protection at checkout, and some are non-refundable. Read the terms before you pay.

Should beginners buy a season pass?

Usually not before they know they enjoy skiing. Try rental and day tickets first, then buy a pass once your pattern is clear.

How do I know if a pass is worth it?

Divide the pass cost by the day-ticket price you would otherwise pay. If you will ski more days than that number, the pass is likely worth it.
