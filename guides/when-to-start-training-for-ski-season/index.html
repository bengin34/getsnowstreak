When to start training for ski season

Start 6 to 8 weeks before your first ski day, with 2 to 3 short sessions a week. Six weeks is enough for most recreational skiers. If you have less time, start anyway: 4 weeks still helps, 2 weeks takes the edge off, and even a few days can make day one easier on your legs.

Updated: 2026-11-02

How many weeks before your first ski day should you start?

Six to eight weeks. That is the rule of thumb German health insurer BARMER gives for beginners and experienced skiers alike[1]. French mountain doctors set the floor a little lower: prepare at least one month before your trip, especially if you don't exercise regularly[2].

So the honest range looks like this:

  • 8 weeks: the comfortable option. Room for a missed week, a cold or a busy stretch at work.
  • 6 weeks: the sweet spot for most people. Long enough to get stronger, short enough that you actually stick with it.
  • 4 weeks: the minimum that still counts as a real block of training.
  • Under 4 weeks: still worth doing, but the goal changes. More on that below.

Starting much earlier than eight weeks is fine, but it rarely pays off for a ski-specific plan. Motivation fades when the first lift is three months away. If you have that much time, build general fitness first (walking, cycling, running) and begin the leg-focused block 6 to 8 weeks out.

What changes in your body week by week?

The reason for six weeks is not arbitrary. Strength comes in two layers.

Weeks 1 to 2: your nervous system learns the moves. Early strength gains come mostly from better coordination and stronger signals from brain to muscle, not from bigger muscles[3]. You feel steadier in a lunge or a single-leg balance long before anything looks different.

Weeks 3 to 4: the muscle itself starts to grow. In one study, thigh muscle size was measurably larger after about three weeks of hard training, and maximal strength rose by almost 40 percent over five weeks[4]. That was a lab program, so don't expect the same numbers from bodyweight work. The timing is what matters: real muscle changes need a few weeks.

Weeks 5 to 6: you build on top. With a base in place, longer intervals and jumping moves make sense. This is where the endurance for a full afternoon on the slopes comes from, and where your legs stop burning after three runs.

Start two weeks before your trip and you only get the first layer. Start six weeks before and you get both.

How often should you train?

Two to three sessions a week. The American College of Sports Medicine recommends 2 to 3 days a week for people new to strength training, with the load raised in small steps[5]. Orthopedic surgeons in Germany suggest the same rhythm for skiers: two to three times a week, about 30 minutes, in the weeks before the season[6].

More is not better when you start late. Training every day in the last two weeks mostly gets you sore legs at the wrong moment. Three sessions with a rest day between them works for most people. What if you only have 4 weeks?

Four weeks is enough for a real effect. You get the coordination gains and the start of muscle growth.

  • Keep 3 sessions a week. Don't add a fourth to catch up.
  • Start at the beginning. Easy intervals in week one, harder ones later. Skipping straight to jumps is how knees and calves complain.
  • Prioritize leg strength and balance: squats, lunges, wall sits, single-leg balance. Those carry over most directly to a ski stance.
  • Make the last week a little easier, not harder.

What if you only have 2 weeks?

Two weeks won't build much muscle. It will teach your body the movements, and that matters more than it sounds. The first time you do a lot of lowering work (squats, downhill walking, skiing), your muscles take damage and get sore. A first bout of that kind of exercise protects the muscle against damage from a similar later bout[7]. So two weeks of squats and lunges means the first ski day isn't the first time your thighs work like that.

  • Aim for 4 to 6 sessions in total, with a rest day after each.
  • Keep the effort moderate. Stop each set with a couple of reps left in the tank.
  • Do your last real session 2 to 3 days before you travel, so any soreness is gone before day one.

What if your trip is just days away?

Then the goal is simple: arrive fresh and ski smart.

  • If you have 4 or more days, one or two easy sessions of squats, lunges and balance work are fine. Keep them short and light.
  • In the last 2 to 3 days, skip hard workouts. Muscle soreness usually peaks a day or two after an unfamiliar workout, which is exactly when you don't want it.
  • On the mountain, take real breaks and watch for signs of fatigue. German orthopedic surgeons list both as basic advice against ski accidents[6].
  • Make the first day shorter than you think. Stop while your legs still feel good, not after they give out.

Keep it going once the season starts

Training doesn't have to stop on the first ski day. If you only ski a few weekends or one trip a year, one or two short sessions a week between trips help you hold on to what you built. If anything hurts during training, especially a knee, stop and get it checked by a doctor or physical therapist before you push on.

Questions

How many weeks before skiing should I start training?

Six to eight weeks before your first day on snow is the usual rule of thumb. Four weeks is a workable minimum. Starting later still helps, just less.

Is 2 weeks enough to get ready for skiing?

Two weeks won't build much muscle, but it gets your legs used to squatting and lowering movements, so the first ski day hurts less. Keep the effort moderate and finish 2 to 3 days before you travel.

How many times a week should I train for skiing?

Two to three sessions a week with a rest day in between. Training every day in the last weeks usually leaves you sore on the trip instead of stronger.

Should I work out the day before skiing?

Not hard. A new or intense workout 1 to 2 days before can leave you sore on your first day. A short walk or light mobility work is fine.

When should I start training for a Christmas ski trip?

For a first ski day around December 19, start in early November for six weeks or late October for eight. Late November still gives you four weeks.
