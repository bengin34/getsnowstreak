Ski cardio training: how much endurance you need

Recreational skiing is moderate cardio with hard bursts, so you do not need to be a runner, but a base of endurance helps you last through the day. Aim for two to three sessions a week for six weeks: one easy 30 to 45 minute effort, one interval session, and leg work that keeps your legs working in rounds.

Updated: 2026-11-04

People often ask whether they need to run before ski season. The honest answer is that skiing is not mainly a cardio challenge, but cardio is what lets you ski a fourth or fifth hour with decent technique. This guide looks at what recreational skiing asks of your heart and lungs, which cardio options work, and a plan that fits around leg strength work.

How hard is recreational skiing on your heart and lungs?

Less than most people think, with some bursts. In a study of six recreational skiers with an average age of 61, skiing sessions of 30 and 75 minutes used about 45 percent of their maximal oxygen uptake on average, while their heart rate reached about 77 percent of its maximum. The authors read the gap as a sign that skiing mixes aerobic and anaerobic effort. Blood lactate stayed low, which suggests the skiers largely controlled their own intensity[1]. It is a very small sample of older skiers, so it cannot speak for everyone, but it fits the experience that skiing is a long, moderate effort with hard moments.

Those hard moments are mostly in the legs. Researchers who measured thigh muscle oxygen in 30 junior racers saw blood volume and oxygen levels fall during giant slalom runs, linked to a high static load that may compromise blood flow to the muscle[2]. In other words, many skiers run out of leg endurance before they run out of breath. That is why cardio training for skiing should include legs that work for minutes at a time, not only steady running.

Does skiing itself improve fitness?

It can. In a randomised study of adults aged 60 to 76, a 12-week guided recreational skiing programme led to higher maximal oxygen uptake, about 15 percent higher adjusted for body weight, than a sedentary control group. Resting lung function values did not change[3]. That is encouraging, but it describes people who skied regularly for 12 weeks, and says little about how you arrive on day one. Pre-season cardio is about making the first days easier, not about replacing skiing.

What kind of cardio helps

Easy steady cardio

Brisk walking, cycling, swimming, rowing or easy running for 30 to 45 minutes at a pace where you can still speak in full sentences. This builds the base that helps you recover between runs and between days. If you have a hilly walk or stairs nearby, use them: they load your legs like skiing does.

Intervals

Short hard efforts with rest between them match skiing's pattern better than a steady jog. Examples: 40 seconds of fast squats, skater steps, mountain climbers or cycling at a hard pace, then 20 seconds of rest, for 3 or 4 rounds. This is also the logic of timed rounds in the ski fitness plan.

Leg endurance work

Wall sits, tempo squats and walking lunges build the ability to keep your thighs working for minutes. Wall sits for skiing and legs burning when skiing explain why this matters.

A six-week cardio layout

WeeksEasy dayInterval day
1 to 230 minutes brisk walk or cycle3 rounds of 30 s hard, 30 s rest
3 to 435 to 40 minutes3 rounds of 35 s hard, 25 s rest
5 to 640 to 45 minutes, include hills or stairs4 rounds of 40 s hard, 20 s rest

Work on top of a week that already has leg strength. If you are doing the full plan, you do not need to add much: the interval rounds in a power-and-endurance session count as your hard day, and a walk or cycle counts as your easy day.

How to judge the effort

You do not need a heart rate monitor. Use a simple talk test. Easy: you can speak full sentences. Moderate: short sentences. Hard: a few words at a time. Most of your cardio time should be easy, with a small share hard. If you are unsure how fit you are at the start, a basic fitness check helps; Suva links a test of flexibility, coordination, strength and endurance alongside its programme[4].

When to start

Six to eight weeks before your first ski day is enough for a recreational skier to build a base. Starting earlier lets you spread the effort over more weeks at lower intensity. When to start ski training has the countdown. If your trip is soon and you have done nothing, do not panic: easy daily walking plus two leg sessions a week still helps.

Altitude, cold and the first day

Resorts are often higher than home, and cold air makes breathing feel harder. Even a fit person feels it on day one. Plan shorter first days, drink water, and stop when you are tired, because tired skiers fall more. Sore after skiing covers recovery.

Safety

If you are over 40, have a heart or lung condition, a history of chest pain, dizziness, or take medication that affects heart rate, talk to a doctor before starting hard intervals. Stop any session for chest pain, unusual breathlessness, or dizziness. Suva states its programme is designed for healthy people and recommends consulting a doctor if you are unsure about your health[4].

Cardio mistakes to avoid

  • Doing all your cardio hard. Most of it should be easy so you can still do leg work two days later.
  • Skipping cardio because you hate running. Cycling, swimming, rowing or a fast walk with a backpack all count.
  • Training hard in the last week. The last seven days before a trip should be easy so you arrive fresh.
  • Ignoring the first-day pacing. Fitness helps, but a short first day with more breaks is the cheapest endurance trick there is.

Cardio does not need to be exciting. A steady habit of three sessions a week for six weeks beats an ambitious plan that you drop after ten days.

Questions

Do I need to be a runner to ski?

No. Recreational skiing is mostly moderate effort with bursts. A mix of easy cardio, short intervals and leg endurance work is enough for most people.

How much cardio should I do before ski season?

Two to three sessions a week for six weeks: one easy 30 to 45 minute session, one interval session, and leg strength work. Add more if you are starting from low fitness.

Is running good training for skiing?

It helps your general endurance, but it does not train the held, bent-knee stance of skiing. Combine it with wall sits, squats and interval rounds.

Why do my legs tire before I run out of breath?

Skiing keeps the thigh muscles tense for long periods, which can limit their blood flow. Leg endurance often becomes the limit before your heart and lungs.

Is interval training safe for beginners?

For healthy people, short low-impact intervals are fine. If you have a heart or lung condition, are unsure about your health or feel chest pain or dizziness, see a doctor first.
