Ski core workout: a 15-minute routine for stable turns

A ski core workout trains your trunk to resist bending and twisting while your legs work: planks, side planks, dead bugs and bird dogs, 15 minutes, three times a week. Research on trunk training in skiers is limited, so treat it as sensible preparation rather than guaranteed protection.

Updated: 2026-11-04

When people say "core" for skiing, they usually mean the muscles that keep your trunk steady while your legs and skis move underneath it: abdominals, obliques, deep back muscles, glutes. A good ski core workout is not about a six-pack. It is about holding your pelvis and spine in position when a turn pushes you sideways and the snow bumps you forward. This guide gives you a 15-minute routine you can do at home, with honest notes on what research shows and where it stops.

Why does the core matter for skiing?

On skis your legs do the visible work, but the trunk connects them to the rest of you. In a turn, the outside ski pushes up into your body while your torso stays more or less facing downhill, so spine and hips must resist bending and twisting. Researchers studying back overuse injuries in ski racers wrote that better core stability, or lumbar corsets, might help control sideways bending in giant slalom. They noted this as a possible measure, not a proven one[1].

The strongest practical evidence comes from a programme for youth racers. The ISPAInt programme is about 20 minutes long and combines hamstring work, single-leg squats and trunk exercises (dynamic planking and deadbug bridging). Over 12 months, racers who used it had fewer overuse injuries than a historical control group, and lower-back overuse in the previous two weeks was lower (1.0% against 4.7%)[2]. The authors are clear that the control group was not randomised and that trials are needed. Also, it was a mixed programme, so you cannot credit the trunk part alone. And these are teenage racers, not adults skiing a week a year.

A third piece: researchers use deadbug bridging as a screening test for trunk control in young skiers, measuring how far the pelvis drops sideways. In 101 skiers aged 6 to 15, girls under 15 did better than boys, and body size had only a limited influence[3]. That tells us the exercise is measurable and meaningful for skiers, not that it prevents anything in adults.

So the sensible reading: core work is a plausible, low-risk piece of ski preparation, not a magic fix. It sits beside legs and balance in the ski fitness plan.

What kind of core training works for skiing?

Skiing asks your trunk to resist movement more than create it. That favours exercises where you hold a position while limbs move: planks, side planks, dead bugs, bird dogs. Sit-ups and crunches train spine flexion, which you rarely need on snow, and many people already do too many of them. You can keep a few, but they should not be the main course.

Think in three jobs:

  • Anti-extension: do not let your lower back arch (planks, dead bugs).
  • Anti-lateral flexion: do not let your hip or ribs drop sideways (side planks).
  • Anti-rotation: do not twist when something pulls you (bird dogs, band presses).

The 15-minute ski core workout

Do it on a mat, 3 times a week. Use 30 seconds of work and 30 seconds of rest per exercise, and go through the circuit twice. Beginners stay at 30/30; when it feels easy, move to 35/25 and 40/20. Stop an exercise if you feel sharp pain, especially in the back.

  1. Forearm plank. Elbows under shoulders, body straight, ribs down, glutes tight. If your hips sag, drop to your knees or shorten the time. Quality over minutes.
  2. Side plank. On one forearm, hips lifted in a line from head to feet. Bend the lower knee if needed. Repeat on the other side. This is the closest match for what the trunk does when you load an outside ski.
  3. Dead bug. Lie on your back, arms up, knees bent over hips. Lower the opposite arm and leg slowly while your lower back stays flat on the floor. Return and switch. The moment your back arches, you went too far.
  4. Dead bug bridge. Lift your hips into a bridge, then lift one foot slightly while the pelvis stays level. This is a gentler cousin of the deadbug bridging test used with young racers[3].
  5. Bird dog. On hands and knees, reach one arm and the opposite leg long, hold two seconds, return. Do not let the pelvis rotate.
  6. Mountain climbers (slow). From a high plank, drive one knee toward your chest, then switch. Slow and controlled is the aim; speed comes later. It adds a heart-rate challenge for the final minutes of a ski day.

Fit it before or after a leg session, or on its own on an off day. If you are short on time, do the first three exercises only.

How to progress without hurting your back

  • Make it longer first, then harder. Hold a plank 20 seconds with good form before you try 60 with sagging hips.
  • Add a limb lift. Lift one foot in a plank, or a top arm in a side plank, for 5 seconds.
  • Add resistance. A light elastic band for a standing press out (anti-rotation) works well. See ski training with resistance bands.
  • Add instability last. Feet on a cushion or a sliding towel, never at the start.

Pair it with leg work such as squats for skiing and with balance training from ski balance exercises. Your trunk and legs train together on snow, so train them together at home too.

Common mistakes

  • Holding your breath. Breathe steadily; bracing should not stop breathing.
  • Arching the lower back in a plank. It looks strong and loads the spine in the wrong place.
  • Doing 200 crunches and calling it done. It hits one small part of the job.
  • Training through back pain. A dull muscle burn is fine. Sharp, shooting or radiating pain is not. See a doctor or physiotherapist, especially if you have had back problems before.

Where this fits in your season

Start six weeks before your first ski day, with two or three short core blocks a week next to legs and balance. In season, keep one block going if you can: it takes minutes and keeps the habit alive. For the full timeline, read when to start ski training.

Questions

What is the best core exercise for skiing?

Side plank and dead bug are good picks, because they train the trunk to stay stable while limbs move or the body is loaded sideways.

Are sit-ups good for skiers?

They are not harmful for most people, but they train spine flexion, which skiing rarely needs. Favour planks, dead bugs and bird dogs.

How often should I do ski core training?

Two or three short sessions a week for six weeks before the season is a sensible plan. One session a week in season keeps the habit.

Does core training prevent ski injuries?

It cannot be promised. A mixed programme in youth racers showed fewer overuse injuries versus a historical control, but trunk work was only part of it.

Can I do this core workout if I have back pain?

Check with a doctor or physiotherapist first. Stop any exercise that causes sharp or radiating pain.

Do I need equipment?

No. A mat and floor space are enough. An elastic band is a useful extra for anti-rotation work.
