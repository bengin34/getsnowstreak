Snowboard Wrist Injury Prevention: Guards, Falls and Fitness

Wrist guards are the best-supported way to cut snowboard wrist injuries, and beginners benefit most. Learning to fall without bracing with a straight arm, riding within your level and keeping your legs fresh help too. A wrist that is swollen, deformed or very painful needs a clinician.

Updated: 2026-11-04

Why wrists are the snowboarder's weak point

When you ride with both feet strapped to one board, you cannot step out of a fall. The reflex is to throw a hand out. That puts your whole body weight through the wrist at the moment of impact. In an 18-year study at a Vermont ski resort, wrist injuries were the most common snowboarding injury, while skiers' most common injury was an ACL sprain[1]. A ten-year Colorado survey of 7,430 snowboarding injuries found that about half involved the upper extremity, with the wrist the most common site[2].

Who is most at risk

The Colorado survey found that beginners, women and younger riders had more wrist fractures and sprains[2]. The Vermont data also showed injured snowboarders were younger, less experienced and more often female than injured skiers[1]. Terrain park injuries made up a larger share of snowboarding injuries (21.8%) than of skiing injuries (6.5%)[1]. So risk concentrates around the first days on a board, the first days of a season and jumps.

These are group statistics. They describe who gets hurt more often, not who will get hurt. But they tell you where to spend your attention.

What the research says about wrist guards

A systematic review of six studies concluded that wrist guards significantly reduced wrist injuries, wrist fractures and wrist sprains among snowboarders. It also noted that some non-experimental data suggested a possible increase in finger and elbow or shoulder injuries, and called for more research[3]. In the Colorado survey, riders who wore wrist guards were about half as likely to hurt their wrists[2].

That is a fair summary of the state of things: guards help, the effect is meaningful, and nobody claims they remove the risk. A broader review of skiing and snowboarding injuries describes the evidence on wrist guards as disputed in places[4], which is a good reason not to treat them as magic.

Practical points:

  • Choose guards made for snowboarding. They have a rigid splint on the palm side that stops the wrist bending back too far.
  • Fit matters. A guard that slides around does little. Try them on with your gloves.
  • Wear them early. The first days on a board and the first day of a season are when most beginners fall.
  • Do not let guards make you reckless. They are a safety net, not permission to ride beyond your level.

How to fall better

Good falling is a skill you can practise. These habits are widely taught by instructors:

  • Do not reach out with a stiff arm. Keep elbows bent and close to your body, and try to land on your forearms, hips or padding.
  • Roll rather than brace. Rolling spreads the force over a bigger part of the body.
  • Tuck your chin to protect your head and keep your eyes on the ground ahead.
  • Fall early. If you feel you are losing control, sit down on purpose before you pick up speed.
  • Stay down a moment after a fall, then stand up carefully. Many riders get hurt when they slide to a stop and a second rider hits them.

An instructor can teach this in a single session, and it is probably the best money you can spend in your first week.

Fitness that helps your wrists and shoulders

There is no trial I can point to that shows wrist or forearm exercises prevent fractures, so I will not claim it. What fitness does is reduce falls. A tired rider catches an edge more often and recovers slower. An orthopaedic review of ski injuries recommends pre-season conditioning and stopping before fatigue sets in[5]. A snowboard workout that builds legs and core is the practical way to follow that advice.

  • Legs and hips: squats, lunges and wall sits keep you low and balanced. See squats and lunges.
  • Core: planks and side planks keep your upper body stable over the board. See snowboard core and balance.
  • Single-leg balance: helps with recovery when the board skids out.
  • Gentle wrist mobility such as slow circles and a light stretch can be part of a warm-up. It will not make a fracture less likely, but a warm joint feels better.

A six-week routine of three short sessions per week is a realistic starting point. The structure is in our six-week fitness plan.

Before you ride

  • Warm up for a few minutes: march in place, leg swings, a few squats.
  • Take a lesson on day one, and again if you move to a new type of terrain.
  • Skip the terrain park until you can link turns confidently and control your speed.
  • Stop when you are tired. The last run is not worth a cast.

After a fall: when to see a clinician

This is general information and not a diagnosis. Stop riding and have the wrist examined if you notice any of the following:

  • Visible deformity or a bent shape
  • Swelling that builds, or bruising
  • Severe pain, or pain when you press on the bone at the base of the thumb
  • Numbness, tingling or pale fingers
  • You cannot grip or turn the hand

A wrist that hurts a lot after a fall is not safe to test on one more run. Wrist injuries can look mild at first. An X-ray is the clinician's call, not ours.

Choosing gear and setting up

Gear will not fix technique, but a few choices lower the odds of a hard landing. Gloves with built-in wrist support are an option for people who dislike separate guards, though I have not seen trial data on them, so treat the claim with care. Padded shorts and a helmet are widely recommended by instructors. Whatever you wear, rent from a shop that adjusts it for you and ask them to check that the bindings, stance and boots suit your level.

Conditions matter as much as kit. Icy mornings and crowded slopes lead to more catches of the edge, so on those days stick to gentler terrain, shorten your session and make your last run an easy one.

Questions

Do wrist guards really prevent snowboard injuries?

A systematic review found they significantly reduced wrist injuries, fractures and sprains. Some data hint at more finger or elbow injuries, so they reduce risk but do not remove it.

Who gets snowboard wrist injuries most?

Beginners, women and younger riders had more wrist fractures and sprains in a ten-year Colorado survey. The first days on a board carry the highest risk.

How should I fall when snowboarding?

Keep elbows bent, avoid bracing with a stiff arm, roll or land on forearms and hips, tuck your chin, and fall early if you lose control.

Can exercise prevent wrist fractures?

There is no solid evidence that wrist exercises do. Leg and core fitness may reduce falls caused by fatigue, which is the practical benefit.

When should I see a doctor after a fall?

If the wrist is deformed, swelling builds, the pain is severe, or you have numbness or cannot grip. Do not keep riding on it.
