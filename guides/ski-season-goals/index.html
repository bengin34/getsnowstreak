Ski season goals: how to set ones you will actually hit

Good ski season goals are specific, measurable and few: a number of ski days, one skill target and one fitness target, with a date and a way to check them. Vague goals such as ski more or get better are easy to agree with and hard to act on. Review them monthly and adjust.

Updated: 2026-11-04

Every autumn skiers promise themselves a better winter. By February most cannot say whether they got one. The promise was the problem: ski more, get fitter, finally do that black run. None of it can be checked. This guide shows how to turn the wish into three goals you can measure, plan around and review.

Why specific goals work better

A review of 35 years of goal-setting research reached a consistent conclusion: specific and challenging goals lead to higher performance than vague ones or "do your best" instructions, provided the person has the ability and accepts the goal[1]. The same review points out that goals work through four mechanisms: they direct attention, energize effort, increase persistence and encourage you to find strategies. That matches ordinary experience. A target of 15 ski days makes you look at the calendar in November. Ski more does not.

The word "challenging" needs care, though. A goal that is far beyond your time, budget or body is a goal you will drop. Aim for a stretch, not a fantasy.

Pick three goals, not ten

One in each area covers a season well.

  1. A volume goal: how many days you will ski.
  2. A skill goal: one thing you want to do better.
  3. A body goal: how you will arrive and how you will finish days.

A fourth goal about money fits if you are watching the budget; see below.

Goal 1: Days on snow

Start from your history, not your hopes. If you skied 9 days last year, 14 is a reasonable stretch and 30 is not. Then check the number against your calendar: every weekend, holiday and trip you can already name. Count the days, remove a quarter for weather and illness, and you have a target you can plan. If you are unsure what is typical, how many ski days per season may help.

Your day target is also the basis of every money decision. Whether a season pass pays for itself depends on it, as shown in is a ski pass worth it.

Goal 2: A skill target

Pick one thing and describe it so a stranger could tell whether you did it. Not "improve my carving" but:

  • Ski a named red run without stopping, by the end of January.
  • Link ten parallel turns on a blue run, by the end of the second trip.
  • Take three lessons and film one run from each to compare.

The more concrete the target, the easier it is to tell if you are getting closer. Research on how people track progress found that goals described in quantifiable terms are monitored more often and more easily[2], so make them countable: runs, turns, days, lessons.

Goal 3: A body target

Most skiers aim to arrive fit enough to enjoy the first days. Something like: complete the six-week preparation (three sessions a week) before the first ski day, and be able to hold a wall sit for a set time. The ski fitness plan sets out the sessions, and the ski fitness test gives you a way to measure your starting point so the progress is visible.

Also set a finishing target: you want energy for the last hour of the day, when more injuries tend to happen. See ski afternoon fatigue injuries.

Optional: a money target

If cost matters, express it as a number per day, not a total. For instance: keep the average cost per ski day below a figure you choose. That ties your money goal to your volume goal, so skiing more lowers the number instead of just spending more. Details are in ski cost per day and ski season budget.

Review monthly and adjust

A meta-analysis of 138 randomized studies found that monitoring progress toward goals increased the chance of reaching them, with a larger effect when progress was recorded physically or made public[3]. So put a date in your calendar on the first of each month. Ask three questions: where am I against each goal, what is getting in the way, and is the goal still right?

It is fine to change a goal. If a knee injury ends your season, replace the skill target with a recovery one. A goal that cannot be reached any more is not a motivator. Changing it deliberately is better than ignoring it silently. And if pain or swelling persists after a fall, see a doctor or physiotherapist before you set new targets.

A ski day log gives you the numbers for the review without extra work.

Common mistakes

  • Too many goals. Three is enough.
  • Goals that depend on others. Make sure you control the outcome.
  • Goals with no date. Without a deadline there is no pressure to start.
  • No plan for the first step. Decide what you do this week.

An example of how goals shape a month

Say your target is 14 ski days with one skill goal, a named red run without stopping by the end of January. In November you book the two trips and count the weekends, which shows you can reach 11 days with ease and need three more. In December you decide to use two of the three remaining days on lessons, since the skill goal needs them. In January the log shows you have skied seven days and your ratings are higher on days when you stopped before your legs went. You move the red run attempt to a morning, not the end of the day. None of this happens with the goal ski more, because nothing in it can be compared with a calendar.

Questions

How many ski days should I aim for in a season?

Start from last season's number and add a modest stretch, then check it against your actual calendar. A target you can plan around beats an ambitious one you will abandon.

What is a good skill goal for skiing?

One concrete, checkable thing, such as skiing a named run without stopping or linking ten parallel turns, with a date attached.

How many goals should I set?

Three is a good number: days, one skill and one fitness target. Add a cost-per-day target only if money matters to you.

How often should I review my ski goals?

Once a month is enough. Check progress, find what is blocking you and adjust the goal if it no longer fits.

What if I get injured and can't reach my goals?

Change them on purpose. Replace the skill or volume target with a recovery goal, and see a doctor or physiotherapist if pain or swelling persists.
