Ski stretching routine: 7 stretches for after skiing

A good ski stretching routine takes about 10 minutes and covers calves, quads, hip flexors, hamstrings, glutes and inner thighs. Do it after skiing or training, not as a long hold right before you ski. Stretching improves range of motion, but evidence says it does little for soreness.

Updated: 2026-11-04

Skiing is not a flexibility sport. You do not need the splits, but tight calves, hips and quads make a deep, balanced stance harder to hold and a long day more tiring. A short stretching routine after training or after the lifts closes at the end of the day is a sensible habit. Before you ski, keep it dynamic and brief.

Does stretching help you ski better?

It helps your range of motion, and that is mostly it. A 2023 meta-analysis of 77 studies found that regular stretch training increases range of motion by a moderate amount compared with no stretching, and that static and PNF stretching gain more than ballistic stretching[1]. The same review found no clear difference between stretching more often, longer or harder, so a short routine done consistently is a fair bet.

What stretching does not do is protect you the way people assume. The Cochrane review on stretching and muscle soreness pooled 12 studies, including one field trial with 2,377 people. Stretching before or after exercise changed next-day soreness by about one point on a 100-point scale, which the authors call clinically unimportant[2]. If your goal is less soreness the next morning, see why you are sore after skiing and what helps.

The timing matters too. A 2016 systematic review by Behm and colleagues found that static stretching right before an effort lowered performance by about 3.7 percent on average, and by 4.6 percent when each muscle was held for 60 seconds or more. Dynamic stretching slightly raised performance, by about 1.3 percent. The range-of-motion gain from a stretch typically faded within 30 minutes[3]. So for the chairlift queue, use dynamic movement. Keep long holds for later.

When to stretch and when to move instead

  • Before skiing: a few minutes of leg swings, easy squats and ankle circles. Our guide on warming up before skiing has a full sequence.
  • After skiing or training: the routine below. Muscles are warm, you are not about to load them hard and 10 minutes is enough.
  • On rest days: optional. A walk does as much for stiff legs as a long stretching session.

The 10-minute ski stretching routine

Hold each static stretch for 20 to 30 seconds, two rounds per side. Stretch to a firm pull, never to pain. Breathe out slowly and let the muscle ease off rather than forcing the position. Skip any stretch that pinches a joint.

1. Calf stretch against a wall

Hands on the wall, one foot back with the heel down and the leg straight. Lean forward until you feel a pull in the lower calf. Then bend the back knee slightly to reach the deeper calf muscle and the Achilles area. Ski boots lock your ankles in place, so calf length decides how far forward your shin can travel in a stance. For more on this, see ankle mobility for skiing.

2. Quad stretch standing

Hold a wall for balance. Bend one knee and pull the heel toward your glute, keeping the knees together and the hips level. Do not arch your lower back. You should feel the pull along the front of the thigh, not in the knee. Quads do most of the braking when you ski, so they are the muscle that feels it most.

3. Hip flexor lunge stretch

Kneel on one knee, cushioned, with the other foot flat in front. Tuck your pelvis slightly under and shift your weight forward until the front of the back hip stretches. Raise the arm on the same side as the back leg for a longer line. Hours in a flexed ski position and a desk job both shorten this area. More ideas in hip mobility for skiing.

4. Hamstring stretch, one leg on a step

Put one heel on a low step with the leg straight and toes up. Hinge forward from the hips with a flat back until you feel the pull behind the thigh. Do not round the back to reach further. If you feel it behind the knee more than in the muscle, ease off.

5. Figure-four glute stretch

Lie on your back with knees bent and feet flat. Cross one ankle over the opposite knee, then pull the lower thigh toward your chest. You should feel the stretch in the outer hip and glute of the crossed leg. This one is easy on the lower back.

6. Adductor stretch, wide kneeling

On hands and knees, slide your knees apart as far as is comfortable and rock your hips gently back toward your heels. Stop when you feel a pull along the inner thighs. Skiers use the inner thigh to edge and to stay balanced from turn to turn.

7. Child's pose with side reach

Sit back on your heels with arms forward on the floor, then walk both hands to one side to stretch the opposite side of your back and torso. Breathe into the stretched side. This unwinds the trunk after long runs and falls.

How often should you do it?

Two to four short sessions a week is plenty. Because the review found no clear advantage for stretching more often or for longer[1], extra time is better spent on strength and balance. Stretching sits alongside those, it does not replace them. If you are building a full pre-season plan, start from our ski fitness plan, and use the stretches above at the end of your sessions.

Pair it with exercises that actually build the position you ski in. Lunges and squats train strength through a range of motion, so they improve how usable your flexibility is. You can find examples in our guide to ski exercises at home.

Who should be careful

Stretching should never hurt in a sharp or lasting way. If you have an old injury, a hypermobile joint, a recent strain or pain that does not ease within a few days, talk to a doctor or physiotherapist before you push range. Sudden pain in the knee after a fall or twist is a reason to get it examined, not to stretch it. Flexibility alone does not make a stiff knee safe, and the research here is mostly on healthy adults rather than skiers specifically.

A short realistic plan

If you only do one thing, do the calf, quad and hip flexor stretches after each training session or ski day. That is about four minutes and covers the areas that boots and a flexed stance tighten most. Add the rest when you have time.

Questions

Should I stretch before skiing?

Use short dynamic movements such as leg swings and easy squats instead of long static holds. Static stretching right before effort lowered performance slightly in a 2016 review.

How long should I hold each ski stretch?

About 20 to 30 seconds, two rounds per side, to a firm pull and never to pain. A recent meta-analysis found no clear benefit from holding longer.

Does stretching stop sore legs after skiing?

Not much. The Cochrane review found stretching changed next-day soreness by about one point on a 100-point scale, which is not clinically important.

Which muscles matter most for skiers?

Calves, quads, hip flexors, hamstrings, glutes and inner thighs. Boots limit ankle motion and the flexed stance loads the quads and hips.

How often should I stretch for skiing?

Two to four short sessions a week is enough. Spend the rest of your time on strength and balance, which stretching cannot replace.
