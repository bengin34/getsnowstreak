Ski knee injury prevention: what actually protects your ACL

Most serious ski knee injuries are ACL tears from a twisting fall, often while sitting back. You lower your risk with three things: training single-leg control before the season, bindings set by a shop, and knowing the moves that load the knee. Nothing makes a knee injury-proof.

Updated: 2026-11-02

How do knee injuries happen when you ski?

The knee is the weak link in skiing. ACL sprains remain the most common injury on skis, and modern bindings do little to stop them: they are built to protect your shin from bending and twisting fractures, not your knee[1]. That is why you can't leave knee safety to the gear.

Researchers in Vermont have studied ski injuries for decades. They describe two scenarios behind most ACL tears in recreational skiers[2].

Phantom foot

You get off balance to the rear, your hips drop below your knees, your uphill arm swings back and your weight lands on the inside edge of the downhill ski tail. The tail acts as a lever against the stiff back of the boot and twists the knee. It often happens when you try to stand up while still sliding or try to save a fall that is already lost[2].

Boot-induced

You land a jump or a drop back-weighted. The tail hits first, the boot cuff pushes the shinbone forward under the thighbone and the ACL tears[2]. You don't need a park kick for this. A roller you didn't see is enough.

Slip-catch and the MCL

In ski racing, the most common pattern is the slip-catch: the outside ski loses grip, then the edge suddenly bites again and forces the knee inward and into rotation[3]. Recreational skiers know a slower version: a caught edge in a snowplow or on flat light that pulls the knee into a knock-kneed position. That inward load is the typical way to sprain the MCL, the ligament on the inside of the knee.

Can training prevent knee injuries in skiing?

It can help, but be careful with big promises. Here is what the evidence says.

  • Awareness works in skiing. Ski patrollers and instructors at 20 resorts watched video of real knee injuries and learned to spot the phantom foot position. Their serious knee sprains dropped 62% compared with the two seasons before. Resorts without the training saw no drop[4].
  • Single-leg training looks promising. Swedish ski high schools added a program with a video plus single-leg hops, single-leg squats and on-snow drills, done equally well on both legs. ACL injuries fell from 8.1% to 3.9% of skiers. The comparison used earlier students, not a randomized control group, so the real effect is uncertain[5].
  • Neuromuscular programs work in other sports. Across many trials, balance, strength and landing programs cut ACL injuries by about half, with the clearest data in female athletes[6]. Nobody has tested them properly in skiing yet[3].

So training is a reasonable bet, not a guarantee. What it can do: give you the strength to stay centered, recover from a bad position and control your knee over your foot on one leg.

Which exercises protect your knees for skiing?

Focus on control, not just big thigh muscles. A good knee block trains one leg at a time and side-to-side movement, because skiing loads one leg and pushes you sideways.

  • Single-leg balance: 30 seconds per leg, knee soft, then with eyes closed.
  • Single-leg deadlift: hinge at the hip, keep the knee in line with your toes.
  • Lateral lunge: sit into one hip, push back to the middle.
  • Skater step or skater hop: land quietly on one leg and hold for a second.
  • Side plank: a strong trunk keeps your hips over your skis.

Watch your knee in a mirror. If it caves inward on landings, slow down until it stays over your toes. Full instructions and beginner swaps are in ski exercises at home, and the six-week ski fitness plan puts them into a weekly schedule.

Should you set your bindings lower to protect your knees?

No. The release value, often called the DIN setting, comes from your weight, height, boot sole length, age and skier type. A ski shop sets it and tests the release with a calibrated device. Don't turn the screws yourself, and don't copy a friend's number.

Lowering it does not make your ACL safer. The ACL injury mechanism is not related to binding function, and lower settings mostly add pre-releases, where the ski comes off at speed and you fall anyway[1]. What a correct setting does protect is your shin. So have your bindings checked every season, after a hard crash and whenever you change boots. At the rental desk, give your real weight and ability.

Are tired legs the main risk?

Not as clearly as most people think. In a study of women with ACL tears, about 81% got injured within the first three hours of skiing, and most said their legs felt barely tired at the time[7]. The first runs of the day count as much as the last ones.

That doesn't mean you should push through burning thighs. Tired legs make it harder to stay forward, and sitting back is the start of the phantom foot. Warm up before the first run, ski an easy slope first and stop when you notice you're getting sloppy. If your legs give up early every day, read why your legs burn when skiing.

What should you do on the slope?

The Vermont group boils its advice down to a few habits[2]:

  • Keep your arms forward and your hands over your skis. When your hands drop behind you, your weight follows.
  • Keep your hips above your knees. If you end up sitting back, don't fight it with a twist.
  • If you fall, keep your knees bent and stay down until you stop sliding. Don't try to stand up while moving.
  • Don't jump unless you know where and how to land, and land on both skis.
  • Ski terrain and speed you control, especially in flat light, on ice and in heavy snow.

A lesson is worth it if you spend a lot of time in the back seat. A ski instructor can spot it in one run.

When do you need to see a doctor?

If you felt a pop, your knee swells within hours, it gives way when you turn or you can't put weight on it, stop skiing and see a doctor or physical therapist. The same goes for knee pain that doesn't settle after a few days. Don't test it with another run. A clinician can check the ligaments and tell you what's safe.

Questions

What is the most common knee injury in skiing?

ACL sprains and tears are the most common serious ski injury. MCL sprains, on the inside of the knee, are also common, often from a caught edge that pulls the knee inward.

Do bindings prevent ACL tears?

Not reliably. Bindings are built to protect the shin from fractures. Most ACL tears happen in falls where even a correctly working binding doesn't release in time.

Should I lower my DIN setting to protect my knees?

No. A lower setting doesn't protect the ACL and makes pre-releases more likely. Let a ski shop set and test it based on your weight, height, boot sole length, age and skier type.

Does a knee brace prevent ski injuries?

There's no good evidence that a brace protects a healthy knee on skis. If you had knee surgery, ask your surgeon or physical therapist what to wear.

How long before the season should I start knee training?

About six weeks of two to three short sessions a week gives you time to build single-leg control. Starting later still beats arriving untrained.
