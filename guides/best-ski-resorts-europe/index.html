Best ski resorts in Europe: a guide by country

There is no single best ski resort in Europe, but there are clear patterns by country. France has the largest linked areas, Austria and Switzerland have high, snow-sure terrain, and Italy has Dolomite scenery. Below are well-known resorts in each country with their published slope length and altitude, and the kind of skier each suits.

Updated: 2026-11-20

There is no single best ski resort in Europe, and a list that claims one is selling something. What exists is a set of countries with different strengths: big linked areas in France, high and snow-sure terrain in Austria and Switzerland, and Dolomite scenery in Italy. This guide groups well-known resorts by country, gives size and altitude figures from published resort data, and says what each type of resort tends to suit. It does not rank them.

How to read the numbers

Two figures do most of the work. Slope kilometres tell you how much variety a week offers, though a large number does not mean good terrain, and the figure usually counts every linked lift area. Altitude tells you how reliable the snow is likely to be and how long the season lasts; a top station above 3,000 metres behaves very differently from one at 2,000. All figures below come from skiresort.com resort pages and country lists, and they change a little between seasons.

Before you compare resorts, decide what you are optimising for: variety, snow reliability, gentle slopes, a short transfer or a low daily cost. Those goals point to different places.

Austria

Austria is the country of village skiing: wide, rolling runs and a strong lift network, with many resorts at moderate altitude. Sölden has 144.1 km of slopes between 1,350 and 3,340 metres. Mayrhofen lists 142 km between 630 and 2,500 metres. KitzSki, around Kitzbühel and Kirchberg, lists 188 km between 800 and 2,000 metres, and 54 per cent of its slopes are classed as easy.[1] Ischgl and Samnaun, on the Austrian-Swiss border, list 239 km between 1,360 and 2,872 metres.[2]

Suits: mixed groups and intermediates who want long cruising runs, plus anyone who values a village base. A resort with a top at 2,000 metres, such as KitzSki, is best in midwinter; for late season, a resort with glacier terrain such as Sölden is the better fit (see spring skiing resorts).

Switzerland

Switzerland has some of the highest top stations in the Alps. Arosa Lenzerheide lists 225 km between 1,229 and 2,865 metres.[3] The Zermatt-Matterhorn area, linked with Cervinia on the Italian side, lists 322 km between 1,562 and 3,899 metres and has summer skiing on the Theodul glacier.[4]

Suits: skiers who value altitude and snow reliability. For budget comparisons, work from a cost-per-day figure rather than a pass headline; ski cost per day shows how.

France

France is where the largest linked areas are. The Three Valleys, with Val Thorens, Les Menuires, Méribel and Courchevel, list 600 km between 1,100 and 3,230 metres. The Portes du Soleil, which crosses into Switzerland, lists 580 km between 985 and 2,254 metres. Tignes and Val d'Isère list 300 km between 1,550 and 3,456 metres, La Plagne 225 km, and Les 2 Alpes 220 km between 1,280 and 3,523 metres.[5]

Suits: keen intermediates and above who want to cover a lot of different terrain in a week, and anyone who wants high, purpose-built bases. Because the areas are big, a slow first day is normal, and it pays to arrive with trained legs; the ski fitness plan is built for that.

Italy

Italy offers a mix of Dolomite areas with a strong lift network and Western Alpine areas that link into France. Val Gardena lists 181 km between 1,236 and 2,518 metres. Madonna di Campiglio with Pinzolo, Folgarida and Marilleva lists 155 km between 852 and 2,504 metres. Kronplatz lists 125 km between 973 and 2,275 metres. Via Lattea, with Sestriere, Sauze d'Oulx, San Sicario, Claviere and Montgenèvre, lists 400 km between 1,538 and 2,789 metres.[6]

Suits: those who want a compact, scenic area, a long lunch and a mountain-hut culture. Italy and Switzerland meet at Zermatt and Cervinia, which is worth a look if you want both countries on one pass area.

Notice what the table cannot tell you: the quality of the snow on the day, the crowd level, or how well the runs match your level. Use it to narrow down, not to decide.

How to choose for your own trip

  1. Fix your dates first. Early and late season need height; January works almost anywhere. See the best time to ski and early season ski resorts.
  2. Be honest about your level. If you are new to skiing or travelling with beginners, look at best ski resorts for beginners first; a 600 km area does little for someone on the nursery slope.
  3. Count the whole day. Transfer time, lift queues and the cost of a day matter as much as the map. Compare resorts by cost per ski day, not by headline size.
  4. Think about how many days you will actually ski. A week in a huge area and a few days in a small one are different products; how many ski days per season helps set a realistic number.

Where this guide ends

Resort size and altitude are facts you can check. Whether a resort is the right one for you is a judgement that depends on your level, your dates and your group. Take two or three candidates from the lists above and check each resort's own site for its current lift map, season dates and snow report.

Questions

Which country has the biggest ski areas in Europe?

By published slope length, France has the largest linked areas in this guide: Les 3 Vallées lists 600 km and the Portes du Soleil 580 km.

Which European ski country is best for beginners?

It depends on the resort rather than the country. Look for a large share of easy runs, as at KitzSki in Austria, where 54 per cent of slopes are classed as easy.

Where is the highest skiing in Europe?

Of the areas listed here, Zermatt and Cervinia reach 3,899 metres, ahead of Les 2 Alpes at 3,523 metres and Tignes and Val d'Isère at 3,456 metres.

Is a bigger ski area always better?

No. More kilometres mean more variety over a week, but not better snow, better terrain for your level, or a lower cost per day.

When is the best time to ski in Europe?

Midwinter works almost everywhere, while early and late season favour high and glacier resorts. Season dates change each year, so check the resort's calendar.
