Ski training with resistance bands: what works and what to skip

Resistance bands can build leg strength for skiing about as well as weights in the studies we have, and they cost little and travel easily. Use them for banded squats, lateral walks, glute bridges and hamstring work, 2 to 3 times a week, and progress by band tension or slower reps.

Updated: 2026-11-04

Bands are the cheapest piece of gym equipment you can own, and they fit in a ski bag. The honest question is whether they do anything useful for skiing, or whether they are just a nice thing to put on a shopping list. Short answer: they do, if you pick exercises that match what skis ask of your legs and you push them hard enough to feel it.

Do resistance bands build strength?

A 2019 systematic review pooled eight randomized trials that compared elastic bands or tubes with conventional resistance such as machines and dumbbells. For lower-limb strength the pooled difference was small and not statistically significant (SMD -0.11, 95% CI -0.40 to 0.19). The authors concluded that elastic resistance can produce strength gains similar to conventional training[1]. The trials were small and varied, so read that as "bands are a reasonable tool", not "bands are identical to a barbell".

The catch is effort. A band that you stretch a little and lift easily 30 times is a warm-up, not a training stimulus. Strength comes from sets that end close to the point where another clean rep would be hard. If you can do 20 reps without a fight, you need a thicker band, a slower tempo, or a shorter pause between reps.

What do skis ask of your legs?

Skiing is mostly controlled braking. Your quads lengthen under load through every turn, your hips keep your pelvis steady over the outside ski, and your glutes and hamstrings help hold the knee in line. A study of giant slalom racers found the braking phase lasted about twice as long as the push phase[2]. Race pace is not your pace, but the pattern is the same: long, slow, loaded contractions with the knee bent.

That points to four things worth training with a band: quads under a bent knee, hip stabilisers that keep the knee tracking over the foot, glutes, and hamstrings. The exercises below cover those.

Four band exercises for ski legs

1. Banded squat

Place a loop band just above your knees. Stand with feet shoulder-width apart, sit back and down until your thighs are near parallel or as deep as is comfortable, and push your knees gently out against the band. Three seconds down, one up. The band forces your hip muscles to work so the knees do not drift inward. See squats for skiing for depth and form details.

2. Lateral band walk

Band above the knees or around the ankles, knees slightly bent, hips back. Take 10 slow steps to one side, then 10 back. Stay low, keep your toes forward and do not lean. This targets the side of the hip, which keeps your knee stable when the ski loads on one edge.

3. Glute bridge with band

Lie on your back, knees bent, band above the knees. Push your hips up, squeeze the glutes at the top for two seconds and press your knees out against the band. Lower slowly. Do not arch your lower back to get extra height.

4. Band hamstring curl

Anchor a band at a low point such as a door frame, loop it around one ankle and face the anchor. Bend the knee to pull your heel toward your glute, then return slowly. Hamstrings work with your quads to protect the knee, so they are worth training on their own, not just as a by-product of squats.

How many sets, how often?

A workable starting point is 2 to 3 sets of 10 to 15 slow reps per exercise, two or three days a week, with a day off between sessions. Add tension when the last two reps stop feeling hard. Add tempo, not just bands: five seconds down on a squat with a light band is harder than it looks. Slow lowering is also the eccentric work that skiing demands, and eccentric training has been tested specifically in alpine skiers[3].

What bands cannot do

  • Replace jumping and landing. A band does not teach your legs to absorb impact. Plyometrics do that, in small doses (ski plyometrics).
  • Build much maximal strength alone. If you can already squat well, light bands add little. You may need heavier resistance later.
  • Fix your balance. Pair bands with single-leg work (ski balance exercises).
  • Make a worn band safe. Check for nicks and cracks before every session. A snapping band can hurt your face or eyes. Do not anchor it to a loose door.

Bands also have a quirk: tension rises as they stretch, so the end of the movement is the hardest part. Skiing is the reverse, where you are hardest-loaded in the deep, bent-knee position. That is why slow, deep squats and pauses still matter even when you use a band.

Pain, injuries and when to stop

A burning thigh or tired glutes is normal. Sharp pain in a knee, hip or back is not. Stop, and see a physiotherapist or doctor if it persists, especially after a knee injury. Bands do not make a bad squat safe; they only change where the load comes from. Knee-specific prevention is covered in ski knee injury prevention.

A note on skipping the gym

The point of bands is not to be clever. It is to make training easy enough to actually do. Ten minutes in the living room, three times a week for six weeks, will do more than a perfect plan you never start. If a band stays in the drawer, none of this matters.

Questions

Are resistance bands as good as weights for ski training?

In the pooled trials we have, lower-limb strength gains were not meaningfully different between elastic and conventional resistance. Bands work well if you use enough tension and slow reps, but they are less suited to heavy loading or impact training.

Which band exercises are best for skiing?

Banded squats, lateral band walks, glute bridges and hamstring curls. They train the quads, hip stabilisers, glutes and hamstrings, which hold your knee in line under load.

How often should I train with bands before a ski trip?

Two to three sessions a week with a rest day between is a reasonable pattern. Six weeks gives enough time to move up in band tension at least once.

What band strength should I buy?

A set with light, medium and heavy loops is the safest start. Use the lightest one to learn form, then the one where the last two reps feel hard. Replace any band that shows cracks.

Should I use bands if I have a knee problem?

Ask a physiotherapist or doctor first. Bands change how a movement feels but do not make it safe for an injured joint. Stop if you feel sharp or lasting pain.
