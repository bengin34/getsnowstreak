Ski training over 40: how to get fit for the slopes

Over 40 you can still build skiing fitness. Train legs, balance and endurance three times a week, warm up longer and recover longer. In one study of older adults, 12 weeks of skiing training raised thigh muscle thickness 7.1 percent. Check with a doctor first if you have been inactive or have health conditions.

Updated: 2026-11-04

Over 40, you can still get fitter for skiing, and it is worth the effort. The main changes are practical: warm up longer, build up slowly, protect your recovery and train strength, balance and endurance together. You do not need a different sport, only a more patient plan. Older adults who ski gain muscle and tendon quality, and that is a good reason to start.

Can you still get fitter after 40?

Yes, and skiing itself is evidence. In a study of older adults averaging about 67 years, 12 weeks of alpine skiing training raised thigh muscle thickness by 7.1 percent and maximal knee-extensor torque by 13.3 percent, while the 20 controls showed no meaningful change[1]. A companion study of the same group found that patellar tendon stiffness rose by about 14 percent, though the gain was smaller in women, at 9 percent, than in men, at 19 percent[2]. Muscles and tendons keep adapting with age when you load them.

These were supervised, structured programmes in healthy older adults, not weekend holiday skiing, so they show that adaptation is possible, not what your own season will do. They also cover a wide range of ages above 40. What they suggest is that the body at 45, 55 or 65 responds to training, only with less margin for sloppiness.

What changes with age, and what does not

Reasonable expectations, without overstating the science:

  • Recovery takes longer. Two days between hard sessions is a sensible default, not a luxury. See recovery between ski days.
  • Warm-up matters more. Stiff joints and cold muscles feel worse on a first run. Give it 8 to 10 minutes. Our guide on warming up before skiing has a routine.
  • Strength is use-it-or-lose-it. Without resistance work, muscle mass gradually falls through adulthood. Bodyweight training helps you keep what you have.
  • Balance can stay good. In a study of 142 skiers and snowboarders aged 12 to 70, proprioception scores did not differ significantly by age group[3]. Regular snowsport participants appear to keep this skill. That is one study, and it may reflect who stays on the slopes, but it is encouraging.

Your heart and the first days

Skiing is more strenuous than it looks. In ten sedentary men with a mean age of 51, heart rate during leisure skiing averaged about 126 beats per minute, around 75 percent of the maximum, and on the first day 17.9 percent of readings exceeded 85 percent of maximum[4]. A sudden jump from a sofa to full-day skiing is demanding at any age. If you have heart disease risk factors, high blood pressure, are very out of shape or take medication, speak to a doctor before you start training or skiing. This is routine for adults who have been inactive.

The fix is gradual: build a base before the trip, then keep the first ski days short. See your first day back on skis.

A weekly plan for skiers over 40

Three sessions a week of 20 to 30 minutes plus easy cardio, six to eight weeks before the trip. If you can start earlier, check when to start ski training.

  1. Strength day (legs). Squats to a chair, reverse lunges, wall sits, glute bridges, calf raises. 30 to 40 seconds each, three rounds, with proper rest. Control the lowering phase, since braking is what thighs do on skis. Try squats and wall sits.
  2. Balance and knee day. Single-leg balance, single-leg deadlift, side lunge, skater step, side plank. Hold a wall at first. More in ski balance exercises.
  3. Endurance day. Faster bodyweight intervals, or 30 to 40 minutes of brisk walking, cycling or swimming. See cardio training for skiing.

Add a mobility minute for ankles, hips and calves most days. Boots limit ankle motion, so a stiff ankle shifts load up to the knee. Resources: ankle mobility and hip mobility.

Jumping is optional. Plyometric work can be useful, but if your knees or back complain, use fast but non-jumping versions first. Progress is the point, not the exercise list. For the structure, follow the ski fitness plan and adjust the load to your recovery.

Knees, backs and old injuries

Old injuries often matter more than age itself. If you have had an ACL, meniscus, shoulder or back problem, or you have been told you have arthritis, ask your doctor or physiotherapist which movements to avoid. Our guide on protecting your knees skiing covers the general principles. Pain that is sharp, one-sided, swelling, or that lingers beyond a day or two is a reason to stop and get it checked. Training does not replace medical advice.

Strength first, then speed

The most useful priority over 40 is steady leg strength with control. Fast, jumpy or heavy work can wait until your joints are used to the load. Spend the first three weeks on slow tempo, for example three seconds down and one second up, and add pace only if everything feels solid. Slow repetitions are harder than they look, and they build the kind of braking strength your thighs use on every turn. If a session leaves you limping the next day, you did too much, so cut it by a third next time.

Skiing habits that help

  • Stop before you are exhausted. Many late-day mishaps follow fatigue. See afternoon fatigue and injuries.
  • Take a rest day after two or three hard days.
  • Drink water, eat lunch and take breaks, since you tire faster at altitude and in the cold.
  • Ski your level. Fresh legs on a gentle run beat tired legs on a steep one.

The WHO recommends adults of all ages do muscle-strengthening activity regularly alongside 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week[5]. That baseline helps your skiing as well as your health.

Questions

Is it too late to get fit for skiing at 40, 50 or 60?

No. Studies of older adults show muscle and tendon adaptation after 12 weeks of skiing training. Build up gradually.

How many days a week should I train over 40?

Three short sessions of 20 to 30 minutes with rest days between them, plus easy cardio, is a sensible base for six to eight weeks.

Do I need to warm up longer when I am older?

Many people find longer warm-ups help, so 8 to 10 minutes of easy movement before skiing is a good habit. It costs little.

Should I see a doctor before ski training?

If you have been inactive, have heart risk factors, high blood pressure, joint problems or take medication, yes. Skiing raises heart rate to around 75 percent of maximum in sedentary middle-aged men.

Are jumping exercises safe for me?

They can be useful, but they are optional. If your knees or back complain, use fast non-jumping versions and ask a physiotherapist.
