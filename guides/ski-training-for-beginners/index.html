Ski training for beginners: a simple 6-week plan

Beginners need leg endurance, leg strength, balance and some general fitness. Three bodyweight sessions a week for six weeks, starting easy, covers all four at home. No plan is proven to prevent ski injuries, but a trained body skis longer and recovers faster.

Updated: 2026-11-04

If you are new to skiing, or new to exercise, you do not need a gym or an athlete's plan. You need two things: legs that can hold a bent-knee position for a few minutes at a time, and a routine you will keep doing. Six weeks of three short bodyweight sessions a week is a realistic start. Begin easier than you think you need to.

Why beginner skiers should train at all

Skiing is real exercise. A 2026 study measured the energy cost of alpine skiing at about 7.8 METs on average, which is a vigorous level, with a mean heart rate of roughly 153 beats per minute[1]. An older study of ten sedentary men with a mean age of 51 found their heart rate while skiing averaged about 126 beats per minute, around 75 percent of their maximum, and 17.9 percent of readings on day one were above 85 percent of maximum[2]. In plain terms, a full day on snow is hard for an untrained body.

Injury patterns point the same way. In a 2026 report on 4,270 patients at one Chinese ski clinic, people with less than two years of experience mostly had lower-body injuries, while experts had more upper-body ones. Beginner skill level predicted a lower-body injury with an odds ratio of 4.58[3]. That is one clinic and one season, so it is a pattern to respect and not a rule. It does match what beginners tell you, that legs and knees take the load.

An honest caveat: whether a training plan lowers the injury risk of recreational skiers is not well established. A systematic review of injury-prevention advice for recreational skiing and snowboarding found 80 recommendations in 30 articles, and none was about fitness or training[4]. So train for the experience, to ski longer and enjoy it more, not as a guarantee against injury.

What a beginner actually needs

You do not need to build everything at once. Four qualities matter most, and each can be trained at home.

  1. Leg endurance. Holding a bent-knee stance without your thighs giving out. Wall sits and slow squats cover it.
  2. Leg strength. Squats, lunges and glute bridges.
  3. Balance. Single-leg work, because a ski turn is a series of small recoveries.
  4. General fitness. You can talk during a brisk walk, but it takes effort. The WHO recommends 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week for adults, plus regular muscle-strengthening activity[5]. If you are far below that, just getting more regular is a win.

A 6-week starter plan

Three sessions a week with a rest day between them, 20 to 25 minutes each. Use whatever space you have, and a wall.

Weeks 1 and 2: learn the moves

Do each exercise for 30 seconds with 30 seconds rest, three rounds. Squat to a chair, reverse lunge, wall sit, glute bridge and calf raise. Go slowly and keep your knees tracking over your toes. See squats for skiing and lunges for skiing for form tips.

Weeks 3 and 4: add balance

Keep one leg-strength day and add a balance day: single-leg balance, single-leg deadlift (reaching down with a flat back), side lunge and a side plank. This is where beginners often feel wobbly, and it is normal. Hold a wall until it feels easy. The ski balance exercises guide has options.

Weeks 5 and 6: add a little speed

Work a little longer, 40 seconds on and 20 seconds off, and add one day with faster movements such as quick squats and a skater step. Beginners should skip jumping at first. If your knees hurt, stay with the slower versions. Landing is a skill, and it can wait.

Add some cardio and a warm-up habit

Two brisk 20-to-30-minute walks, bike rides or easy jogs per week build the stamina that gets you through afternoon runs. More in cardio training for skiing. Also build the habit of a short warm-up before every session. It makes training safer, and the same routine carries over to the slope, as in warming up before skiing.

How hard should it feel?

Muscle fatigue and a burn in the thighs is normal. Sharp pain, joint pain, dizziness or anything that gets worse as you continue is not. Stop and rest. If knee or back pain lingers more than a few days, or you have a medical condition, see a doctor or physiotherapist before continuing. Mild soreness one or two days after a session is common while your body adapts.

When to start

Six weeks before your trip is the minimum for this plan. If you have more time, you can run it more gently and repeat blocks. For timelines, see when to start ski training. If your trip is close, the last-week guide shows what is still worth doing. For the full structure, read our ski fitness plan, and for first-day tips on snow read your first ski trip.

How to know it is working

You will not see a dramatic change in the mirror. Look for smaller signs: a wall sit that lasts 15 seconds longer, a single-leg balance that stops wobbling, squats that feel lighter, or being able to finish the session without stopping for breath. Write the numbers down in week 1 and again in week 6. A simple baseline is in our ski fitness test, and it is the best motivation you will get.

Three beginner mistakes

  • Starting too hard. Two good sessions beat one session that leaves you unable to walk downstairs for three days.
  • Skipping rest days. You adapt during recovery, not during the set.
  • Waiting until you feel ready. Fit enough is a moving target. A 20-minute session today beats a perfect plan next month.

Questions

How long should a beginner train before skiing?

Six weeks of three short sessions a week is a practical minimum. More time lets you start gentler and repeat blocks.

Do I need a gym to train for skiing?

No. Squats, lunges, wall sits, glute bridges, calf raises and single-leg balance need only floor space and a wall.

Which exercises matter most for a first ski trip?

Wall sits and squats for thigh endurance, lunges and glute bridges for strength, and single-leg balance work. Add brisk walking for stamina.

Will training stop me getting injured?

Nobody can promise that. Evidence on training for recreational ski injury prevention is limited, but fitter legs tire later, which helps you enjoy more runs.

What if an exercise hurts my knee?

Stop, use a smaller range or an easier version, and see a doctor or physiotherapist if the pain lasts more than a few days.
