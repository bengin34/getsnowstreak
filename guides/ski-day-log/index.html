Ski day log: what to record after every day on the snow

A ski day log is a short note you write after each day on the snow: date, place, conditions, a rating and one line about how you felt. Thirty seconds is enough. Over a season it tells you how many days you really skied, what they cost and which conditions suit you.

Updated: 2026-11-04

Most skiers can tell you where they skied last February but not how many days they skied all winter, or what the season cost. Memory rounds up. A log fixes that without much effort, and it is one of the few habits in skiing that costs nothing and pays off by the second month.

Why keep a ski day log at all?

Three reasons, in order of usefulness.

  1. It gives you the real day count. Every money decision in skiing, from a season pass to a new pair of boots, depends on how many days you ski. A log replaces your guess with a number. See ski cost per day and is a ski pass worth it for what to do with it.
  2. It shows patterns. After ten entries you can see which resorts you rate highest, which conditions you enjoy and whether your legs give out on the third day of a trip.
  3. It keeps you honest about training. If your notes say tired legs at 2 pm every time, that is useful input for your preparation next year.

There is also evidence that the act of recording matters on its own. A meta-analysis of 138 randomized studies, with close to 20,000 participants, found that monitoring progress toward a goal made people more likely to reach it, and the effect was larger when progress was physically recorded or made public[1]. A separate review of self-monitoring for physical activity, covering 19 studies, found that interventions that gave feedback on what people recorded did better than ones without feedback[2]. A log you never look at is a diary. A log you review is a tool.

What to write down

Keep the list short, or you will stop. These seven fields cover almost everything useful.

  • Date. Needed for the day count and for spotting back-to-back days.
  • Resort or area. Name it the same way every time, so you can count visits.
  • Conditions. One word is fine: powder, packed, icy, slush, fog, flat light.
  • Rating. A number from one to five. Do not overthink it; your gut after the last lift is the point.
  • Cost of the day. What you actually spent: ticket or pass share, travel, food. Without this you cannot work out your cost per day.
  • Legs and energy. Fresh, normal or wrecked, and at what time of day it changed.
  • One line. The best run, a technique tip someone gave you, a thing to fix.

Do not log lap counts, vertical metres or speed unless you already enjoy numbers. Those fields look rewarding on day one and become chores by week three.

A spreadsheet, a notes app or a paper notebook in the car all work. The best format is the one you will actually fill in while your boots are still on.

How to make it stick

People who track tend to give up when the record feels like work. Research on tracking found that people are more likely to monitor goals when they can think of them in quantifiable terms, and that the process is easier when it fits that kind of thinking[3]. In practice, that means numbers and fixed fields beat free writing.

  • Log at the same moment every day. The car park, the first coffee after skiing or on the train home. Attach it to something you already do.
  • Keep it under a minute. If it takes longer, remove a field.
  • Log bad days too. A day you cut short is data. It is also a day that counts in your cost per day.
  • Review monthly. Add up the days, the cost and the average rating. Two minutes, once a month.

The same logic applies to preparation. A short log of your home sessions turns vague good intentions into a visible record. The ski training habit streak guide explains how to build that routine, and the ski fitness plan gives you something to log.

Using the log through the season

Before the season: use last year's entries to set a realistic day target. Ski season goals covers how to do this without setting yourself up to fail.

During the season: look at your last three ratings and your energy notes before you decide whether to ski a fourth day in a row. If every third day is rated low with wrecked legs, plan a rest day. Ski recovery between days has ideas for what to do on it.

After the season: add up the days and the cost. Write down three lessons. That page is the most valuable one in the whole log, because it is the input for next autumn's budget in ski season budget.

Paper, spreadsheet or app?

Paper is fast but hard to add up. A spreadsheet is easy to total but needs you to open it. A dedicated app can make entry quicker, but only if it is faster than your notes app. Choose by effort, not features. If you pick a spreadsheet, keep one row per day and the columns above; you can calculate totals with a simple sum.

Common logging mistakes

  • Logging only the good days. Your average rating and your cost per day both become fiction if the cold, icy days vanish from the record.
  • Writing essays. Long entries feel productive and get abandoned by February. Short and boring wins.
  • Changing the fields mid-season. If you rename conditions or switch from 1-5 to 1-10, last month's entries stop being comparable.
  • Waiting until the evening. By dinner you will have forgotten the cost and the exact time your legs went. Do it before you drive off.

If you ski with a partner or family, one person can keep the shared log and everyone adds a rating. It takes ten seconds and makes the end-of-season talk much more fun.

Questions

What should I include in a ski day log?

Date, resort, conditions, a one-to-five rating, what the day cost, how your legs felt and one line of notes. Seven short fields are enough.

How long should logging take?

Under a minute. If it takes longer, drop a field. Consistency matters more than detail.

Does tracking ski days really help?

Evidence from goal research suggests that recording progress improves goal attainment, especially when the record is physical or shared. For skiing, it also gives you a real day count for money decisions.

Should I log days I cut short?

Yes. A half day is still a day you paid for, and it tells you something about fatigue or conditions.

Can I use a spreadsheet instead of an app?

Yes. One row per day with the same columns works well and is easy to total at the end of the season.
