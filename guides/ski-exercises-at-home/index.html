Ski exercises at home: 15 moves, no gym, no equipment

You can get your legs ready for skiing at home with bodyweight and a wall. Train three times a week: one block for leg strength, one for balance and knee control, one for power and endurance. Below are all 15 exercises with cues, easier and harder versions, and interval times.

Updated: 2026-11-02

What do your legs actually do on skis?

On skis, your thighs mostly brake. In a study of giant slalom racers, the outside leg spent about one second per turn bending under load, twice as long as the push phase, and muscle activity in that braking phase was close to a maximal contraction[2]. You are not racing, but the pattern is the same on a blue run: bent knees holding you against the turn, again and again.

That is why the burn comes so fast on day one, and why home training should hold positions and lower slowly instead of bouncing through reps. The knee is also where skiers get hurt most: 27 to 41% of all skiing injuries involve the knee[1]. Legs that keep the knee over the foot are worth building before the first chair.

One honest note. A systematic review of injury prevention in recreational skiing and snowboarding found no published recommendations on fitness at all; the evidence covers equipment, education and behavior. The authors still point out that both sports demand strength, endurance and conditioning[3]. Training at home won't make you injury-proof. It makes your afternoon runs feel more like your morning ones.

How do you set up a ski workout at home?

You need a bit of floor space, a wall and a floor where your feet don't slip. No weights, no bands.

  • Warm up for 2 minutes: march in place for 60 seconds, then leg swings for 60 seconds, one hand on the wall.
  • Pick one block below and do its five exercises as intervals: work, rest, next exercise. Repeat for 3 or 4 rounds.
  • Train three times a week and rotate the blocks: strength, balance, power. A rest day between sessions is fine.

The full six-week structure is in our ski fitness plan. This page is the exercise library: how each move works, what to watch, and how to make it easier or harder.

Block A: leg strength exercises

1. Squat

Feet hip to shoulder width, toes slightly out. Sit back and down as if onto a low chair, chest up, knees tracking over your second toe. Go as deep as you can with your heels down. Beginner: squat to a chair and touch it lightly. Advanced: take three seconds on the way down, the braking part that skiing uses most.

2. Reverse lunge

Step one foot back and lower until both knees are close to 90 degrees, then push through the front heel to stand. Alternate legs. Stepping back is easier on the knees than stepping forward. Beginner: hold the wall and shorten the range. Advanced: pause for one second at the bottom.

3. Wall sit

Back flat against the wall, slide down until your thighs are close to parallel with the floor, knees over ankles. Hold. It is the closest home exercise to a long traverse in your ski stance. Beginner: sit higher, around halfway down. Advanced: thighs parallel, then lift one heel at a time.

4. Glute bridge

Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat. Squeeze your glutes and lift your hips until knees, hips and shoulders form a line. Lower slowly. Strong glutes help stop the knee from caving in when you are on edge. Advanced: single-leg bridge with the other leg straight.

5. Calf raise

Stand tall, rise onto the balls of your feet and lower slowly, fingertips on the wall for balance. Calves and ankles fine-tune the pressure in your boots. Advanced: one leg at a time, or with your heels off the edge of a stair.

Block B: balance and knee control

Most home routines skip these, and they are the ones that matter most for your knees. Why that is, and what else helps, is in our guide to preventing knee injuries when skiing.

6. Single-leg balance

Stand on one leg, knee soft, hips level. Switch legs halfway through. Beginner: fingertips on the wall. Advanced: close your eyes, or turn your head slowly from side to side.

7. Single-leg deadlift

On one leg, hinge at the hips and reach your hands toward the floor while the free leg extends behind you. Back flat, hips square. Come up slowly. Beginner: keep the toes of the back foot on the floor like a kickstand. Advanced: go slower and reach lower.

8. Lateral lunge

Start wide. Shift your weight to one side, bend that knee and sit your hips back while the other leg stays straight. Push back to the middle. This trains the sideways load of edging. Beginner: a smaller step and a shallower bend. Advanced: stay low and shift from side to side without standing up.

9. Skater step

Step wide to the side onto one leg, bend that knee and let the other foot cross lightly behind it. Then step across to the other side. Controlled, no jump. It teaches the same knee position as skater hops, without the impact.

10. Side plank

On your forearm, elbow under your shoulder, body straight from head to feet, hips up. Switch sides halfway through. Beginner: knees bent and on the floor. Advanced: lift the top leg.

Block C: power and endurance

Jumps train the quick reactions you need on bumps and in changing snow. Land soft and quiet, knees bent and pointing over your toes. If your landings are loud or your knees fall inward, use the beginner swap.

11. Squat jump

Squat, jump straight up, land softly back into the squat. Beginner swap: fast squat, the same movement at speed without leaving the floor.

12. Skater hops

Jump sideways from one leg to the other, land on the outside leg and let the other foot swing behind. Hold each landing for a beat. Beginner swap: skater step (exercise 9).

13. Lateral line hops

Feet together, hop side to side over a line on the floor, like a strip of tape or a floorboard seam. Small, quick hops on the balls of your feet. Beginner: step over instead of hopping. Advanced: hop on one leg.

14. Mountain climbers

From a high plank, drive one knee toward your chest, then switch legs quickly. Keep your hips low. Beginner: step the feet in slowly. Advanced: go faster while keeping your hips still.

15. Plank

Forearms down, body in a straight line, glutes and stomach tight. Don't let your hips sag. Beginner: knees on the floor. Advanced: lift one foot or one arm for a few seconds at a time.

How long should you work and rest?

Time-based intervals work better than rep counts at home. Everyone works for the same time, at their own pace. Over six weeks the work gets longer and the rest gets shorter:

WeeksBeginnerAdvanced
1-230 s work / 30 s rest, 3 rounds40 s work / 20 s rest, 3 rounds
3-435 s work / 25 s rest, 3 rounds45 s work / 15 s rest, 3 rounds
5-640 s work / 20 s rest, 4 rounds50 s work / 10 s rest, 4 rounds

A session takes about 17 to 22 minutes including the warm-up. Three a week is enough. Only two weeks until your trip to Breckenridge or Whistler? Do the first rows anyway. A few sessions beat arriving cold.

Which mistakes should you avoid?

  • Knees caving in. On squats, lunges and landings, keep the knee over the second toe. That is the habit you want on edge.
  • Rushing the way down. Skiing is mostly braking. Lower slowly.
  • Only doing cardio. Running helps endurance, but it doesn't train bent-knee holds or sideways loads.
  • Skipping balance. Block B feels easy until you try it with your eyes closed.
  • Training through pain. A burn in the muscles is normal. Sharp pain in the knee, hip or back is not. Stop, and see a doctor or physical therapist if it doesn't settle.

Questions

Can you get fit for skiing at home without equipment?

Yes. Squats, lunges, wall sits, single-leg balance and jumps need only your bodyweight, a wall and some floor space. What matters is training strength, balance and endurance, not owning gear.

How often should you do ski exercises at home?

Three times a week is enough. Rotate a strength, a balance and a power session of about 17 to 22 minutes each, with rest days in between.

What is the best single exercise for skiing?

There isn't one. The wall sit is closest to your ski stance, but you also need single-leg balance for knee control and jumps for quick reactions. A mix of all three works best.

How many weeks before the season should you start?

About six weeks gives you time to build up. If you have less time, start anyway. Even a few sessions help on day one.

Should ski exercises hurt?

A burning feeling in the thighs is normal. Sharp or lasting pain in a joint is not. Stop the exercise and see a doctor or physical therapist.
