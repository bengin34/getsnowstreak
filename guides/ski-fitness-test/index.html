Ski fitness test: 4 simple tests to run at home

A ski fitness test at home needs four checks: a wall sit for leg endurance, a 30-second chair stand for leg strength, a single-leg balance and a plank. Run them now, train for six weeks and retest. There are no official pass marks for skiing, so compare yourself with yourself.

Updated: 2026-11-04

You do not need a lab to know if your legs are ready for the slopes. Four simple tests at home, a wall sit, a 30-second chair stand, a single-leg balance and a plank, show your strengths and weak spots in about 15 minutes. None of them predicts how you will ski. They give you a baseline, so you can see whether six weeks of training moved anything.

What a home ski fitness test can and cannot tell you

Be clear about the limits first. No published cut-off says that a wall sit of a certain length makes you safe on skis. What the tests do is show the qualities skiing leans on: leg endurance, leg strength, balance on one foot, and trunk stability. Their value is in repeating them. Test now, train, test again and compare with yourself, not with a chart.

Research on skiing itself is thin on this question. A systematic review of injury-prevention advice for recreational skiing and snowboarding went through 30 articles and found 80 recommendations, but none of them dealt with physical fitness or training[1]. So treat these tests as practical self-checks rather than proven predictors. If you want the full plan after testing, start with our ski fitness plan.

Before you start

  • Warm up for five minutes: march in place, leg swings, a few easy squats.
  • Test on a non-slip floor, in trainers or bare feet, with a wall or sturdy chair close by.
  • Use a phone timer. Write the numbers down with the date.
  • Rest 2 to 3 minutes between tests.
  • Stop if you feel sharp pain, dizziness or chest symptoms. If you have a heart or joint condition, or you are unsure about testing yourself, check with your doctor first.

Test 1: Wall sit (leg endurance)

How: Back flat against a wall, feet about hip-width and far enough forward that your knees sit over your ankles. Slide down until thighs are level with the floor, or as close as feels solid. Hands off your legs. Time it until you must stand or your thighs rise.

Why it matters: Holding a bent-knee position is the core of what thighs do on a long run. Our guide to the wall sit for skiing explains how to progress it.

How to read it: There is no official ski standard. Record your time and use it as a baseline. If your thighs burn out within 30 seconds, that is information in itself, since long runs last much longer. See why legs burn when skiing.

Test 2: 30-second chair stand (leg strength)

How: Use a sturdy chair of normal height against a wall. Cross your arms on your chest. Stand fully up and sit fully down as many times as you can in 30 seconds. Count only complete stands.

Why it matters: It checks how well you can repeatedly lift your own body weight with your legs. Skiers rely on exactly this when they extend out of a turn.

How to read it: It was developed for older adults, so the usual reference tables do not fit a fit adult heading to the slopes. Use it the way you use the wall sit, as your own before and after number. A big rise after six weeks of squats and lunges is a good sign. Details on the exercises are in squats for skiing and lunges for skiing.

Test 3: Single-leg balance (stability)

How: Stand barefoot on one leg with hands on your hips, the other foot just off the floor, not touching the standing leg. Start the timer, hold as long as you can, and stop when the lifted foot touches down or you hop. Do three tries per leg and keep the best. Then repeat with eyes closed if you feel safe, with a wall within reach.

Why it matters: Skiing is a series of balance recoveries on one outside ski. In the best-known reference study of the unipedal stance test, 549 healthy adults in six age groups showed that hold time dropped significantly with age, both with eyes open and closed, and did not differ between men and women[2]. In practice that means a gap between your left and right leg, or a drop compared with your own last result, tells you more than any chart.

How to read it: Compare left and right. If one side is much worse, train that side more. Our guide on ski balance exercises shows how.

Test 4: Forearm plank (trunk endurance)

How: Elbows under shoulders, body in one line from head to heels, hips not sagging or piking. Hold until your form breaks, then stop the clock.

Why it matters: Your trunk links hips and shoulders, so it helps you stay centred over your skis when the snow is rough. See core workout for skiing.

How to read it: In a study of 471 college students, the average prone forearm plank was about 124 seconds for men and 83 seconds for women, and people who trained more than three times a week held longer[3]. Those are averages from one student group, not goals. Treat them as context, and care more about holding clean form than about hitting a number.

Write down a results card

  • Wall sit: ___ seconds
  • Chair stands in 30 seconds: ___ reps
  • Single-leg balance, eyes open: left ___ / right ___ seconds
  • Forearm plank: ___ seconds

Pick the weakest of the four and give it extra attention. If you are not sure when to retest, six weeks later works, since it matches a typical pre-season block. Also keep the rest of your life in mind: general activity counts too. The WHO recommends adults do regular muscle-strengthening activity in addition to 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week[4]. If you are far from that, the first step is just more regular activity, as covered in ski training for beginners.

What these tests do not cover

They do not test your technique, your knees under real ski forces or your endurance across a full day on snow. A good score is not a promise that you will avoid injury. If you have pain in a knee or hip during a test, stop and see a doctor or physiotherapist before loading it further.

Questions

How do I test if I am fit enough to ski?

Run a wall sit, a 30-second chair stand, a single-leg balance and a plank, then repeat after six weeks of training. No test guarantees you will ski safely, so use them as a baseline.

How long should I hold a wall sit for skiing?

There is no official standard for skiing. Record your time, train, and aim to beat it. Under 30 seconds shows your thighs fade quickly.

Is a single-leg balance test useful for skiers?

It is a quick check of stability, and a gap between your left and right leg is worth training. Hold time falls with age in healthy adults, so compare with your own past result.

How often should I retest?

Every six weeks is enough, in line with a typical pre-season block. Testing weekly mostly measures how tired you are.

Can a fitness test predict a ski injury?

No. These tests show strength, balance and endurance, not injury risk. Research on fitness and recreational ski injury prevention is limited.
