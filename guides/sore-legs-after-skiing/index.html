Sore legs after skiing: what helps, what doesn't, and when to worry

Sore legs after skiing are delayed onset muscle soreness: your quads brake in every turn, and that braking work causes small muscle damage that peaks 48 to 72 hours later. Light movement, sleep and food help most, massage helps a little, stretching doesn't. Sharp pain, swelling or dark urine are not normal soreness.

Updated: 2026-11-02

Why are your legs so sore after the first ski day?

Because skiing works your thighs in a way almost nothing else in daily life does. In every turn your quads brake. The knee bends under load and the muscle lengthens while it holds you up. That is called an eccentric contraction. A study of giant slalom racers found the braking phase of each turn lasted about twice as long as the push phase, and muscle activity during it came close to a maximal effort[2]. You do a slower version of the same thing hundreds of times a day.

Eccentric work and unfamiliar movement are exactly what cause delayed onset muscle soreness, or DOMS. Tiny areas of damage inside the muscle fibers trigger a local inflammatory response, and that response is what hurts[1]. It is not lactic acid. Lactate clears within an hour or so after you stop. The soreness you feel two days later is the repair process.

How long does muscle soreness after skiing last?

DOMS is delayed by design. You often feel fine at dinner on day one, stiff when you wake up, and worst on the morning of day three. Soreness typically peaks 48 to 72 hours after the exercise[1], then fades over the following days. Most people are clear within about a week.

That timing explains a common ski week pattern: day one feels great, day two is stiff, day three hurts on the stairs to the gondola. Strength and coordination also drop while you are sore[1][3], so your legs are weaker than they feel during the first runs of that day.

What actually helps sore legs after skiing?

There is no trick that makes DOMS disappear. Some things reduce it a little, some only feel nice, and a few do nothing measurable. Here is what the research says.

Light movement

Easy movement is one of the most reliable ways to ease the pain[3]. An easy walk through the village, a slow ride on a hotel bike, or the 2-minute warm-up before you click in: march in place, swing your legs. The relief is real but temporary[3]. A meta-analysis also found active recovery reduced soreness compared with doing nothing[4].

Sleep and food

There is little research on sleep and DOMS specifically. Still, repair happens while you rest, and a short night after a long après-ski is the opposite of what your quads need. Eat a proper meal with protein and drink water. Boring, cheap, and you have nothing to lose.

Massage and foam rolling

Massage came out as the most effective technique for soreness in one large meta-analysis[4]. Foam rolling helps less: in a separate meta-analysis it reduced perceived muscle pain by about 6 percent, a small effect, and the authors called its overall benefit for recovery minor[5]. If rolling feels good, do it gently for a few minutes. Don't grind into a muscle that is already tender.

Cold, heat and compression

Cold-water immersion gave a small reduction in soreness, and whole-body cryotherapy helped only when measured within hours of exercise, not a day later[4]. An older review found no clear effect of cold on DOMS at all[3]. Compression garments showed a moderate effect[4]. A hot tub or sauna is not well studied for DOMS. If it relaxes you, fine, just don't expect it to fix anything.

Stretching

Stretching does not reduce soreness[3][4]. Gentle stretching is fine if it feels good. Hard stretching on a sore muscle won't speed anything up.

Painkillers are a question for your pharmacist or doctor, not a ski blog. Research on them is mixed[3], and masking pain on the slope is not the goal.

Can you ski on sore legs on day 2 and 3?

Usually yes. Mild to moderate soreness is not a reason to stay in the chalet, and moving tends to ease it[3]. But plan the day around weaker legs:

  • Start with two or three easy blue runs before anything steep or bumpy.
  • Take more breaks and stop before your legs start to shake. Most falls happen when you are tired.
  • Skip the last run of the day. That is the classic one.
  • If one leg hurts far more than the other, or the pain is sharp, stop.

Reducing intensity for a day or two after heavy soreness is what sports scientists recommend for athletes too[3]. On a ski week that means a shorter day, not a lost day.

Why do you get less sore as the week goes on?

A muscle that has been sore from a certain kind of work gets protected against the same work for weeks afterwards. Researchers call it the repeated bout effect[6]. Your nervous system recruits the muscle more evenly, and the muscle and its connective tissue adapt[6]. That is why day five usually hurts less than day three, even though you ski more.

The useful part: you don't have to earn that protection on the mountain. Squats, lunges, wall sits and other leg work in the weeks before your trip give your quads the same warning. Introducing eccentric and new movements gradually over 1 to 2 weeks is the classic advice for avoiding heavy DOMS[3]. A few weeks of short sessions is enough to make the first day feel very different. Our pre-season ski fitness plan lays that out, and when to start ski training helps you count back from your first ski day.

Soreness the next day is a different problem from thighs burning while you ski. The burn is fatigue during the run, and endurance work helps with that one.

When is it not normal muscle soreness?

DOMS is a dull, spread-out ache in muscles you used. It feels worse when you start moving and better once you are warm. These signs point to something else. See a doctor:

  • Sharp pain in one spot, a pop you felt or heard, or pain that started suddenly during a run. That suggests a strain, or a knee or ligament injury.
  • Swelling of a joint, or one calf that is swollen, warm and painful.
  • Dark, cola-colored urine together with heavy muscle pain or weakness. That can be rhabdomyolysis, a serious breakdown of muscle after extreme exertion that needs medical care the same day[7].
  • Pain that gets worse after day three instead of better, or lasts beyond a week.

When in doubt, ask the doctor at the resort or a clinician at home. Knee pain in particular deserves a look. Our guide on preventing knee injuries when skiing covers the warning signs there.

Questions

Why do my legs hurt more on day 2 or 3 than right after skiing?

Muscle soreness is delayed. The repair process behind it builds over a day or two and typically peaks 48 to 72 hours after the exercise.

Is it lactic acid?

No. Lactate clears within about an hour after you stop. The soreness comes from small muscle damage and the inflammation that repairs it.

Should I ski with sore legs?

Mild to moderate soreness is usually fine to ski on. Start with easy runs, take more breaks and stop earlier, because sore muscles are also weaker. Sharp or one-sided pain is a reason to stop.

Do foam rolling, ice baths or stretching help?

Massage helps the most, foam rolling and cold water only a little, and stretching not at all. None of them make soreness go away.

How do I avoid being sore on my next ski trip?

Train your legs in the weeks before the trip with squats, lunges and wall sits. A muscle that has done similar work recently gets much less sore, which researchers call the repeated bout effect.

When should I see a doctor?

With sharp pain, a pop, a swollen joint or calf, dark cola-colored urine with heavy muscle pain, or pain that gets worse after day three or lasts beyond a week.
