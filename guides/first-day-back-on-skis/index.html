First day back on skis: how to ease into the season

On your first day back, ski less than you think you can: warm up, start on easy runs, take breaks early and stop when your turns get sloppy. Your legs are not fully ready after months off, and falls are the main way people get hurt on the snow.

Updated: 2026-11-04

The first ski day of the season is a mix of excitement and bad judgment. The snow looks perfect, the lift line moves, your friends are already at the top. Your legs, however, have not made a hundred controlled braking turns since last March. This guide covers how to use that first day well, and what to do beforehand and afterwards.

Why the first day deserves a plan

Skiing is mostly eccentric work: your quads lengthen under load through every turn. Time off reduces how well your legs tolerate it, and soreness from it is largest when the activity is new again. A 2026 systematic review on delayed-onset muscle soreness concluded that regular training reduces soreness through repeated-bout adaptation[1]. The flip side is that your first day back is, for your legs, close to a first day.

Injury data fit the same caution. A 2026 meta-analysis of 82 studies put the pooled injury rate for amateurs at 1.9 per 1,000 skier-days, with falls the leading mechanism, though the studies varied a great deal (I² above 99%)[2]. That is a pooled number across very different resorts and skiers, not a personal risk estimate, but it supports a simple rule: fewer falls means fewer injuries, and tired, rusty skiers fall more. I did not find a rigorous study that isolates the first day of the season, so treat the advice below as cautious practice rather than a proven protocol.

Before you go

  • Get your legs ready. Three sessions a week for six weeks is the plan this site recommends. See the ski fitness plan and when to start ski training. If you are short on time, the one-week version is in ski fitness one week before your trip.
  • Check your gear. Boots that fit, bindings tested by a shop if they are due, goggles, helmet that is not damaged or past its life, gloves. Rental skis and boots should be fitted, not just sized.
  • Read the conditions. Early season often means thin cover, rocks, icy patches and crowded open runs. Pick your day when you can.
  • If you are over 40 or have a heart or lung condition, talk to a doctor about exertion at altitude. A review of sudden cardiac death in skiers found that weekly high-intensity exercise was associated with lower risk (OR 0.17), and that a previous heart attack was the strongest risk factor[3]. That is observational and not a prescription, but it is a reason to arrive fit and to take chest pain or unusual breathlessness seriously.

The first 15 minutes

Do not click in cold. An international consensus statement on warm-up and cool-down in competitive skiers noted that warm-up improves performance and lowers injury risk in other sports, but that evidence in snow sports is largely lacking[4]. So there are no ski-specific trial numbers to quote, but a short warm-up is cheap and the logic is sound. Two minutes of marching in place and leg swings, then a few squats and ankle rocks, is enough. A longer routine is in ski warm-up before skiing.

Then ride up and make your first run green or easy blue, even if you ski black. Use it to feel your edges, your balance over the boot tongue and the snow. Your legs and your eyes both need to recalibrate.

Pace the day

  1. Morning: three or four easy runs, then a short break.
  2. Mid-morning: add a slightly harder run if your turns feel good. Stay on pistes you know.
  3. Lunch: eat and drink properly. Sit for 45 minutes, not 10.
  4. Afternoon: shorter. Plan to finish earlier than you did last season. Fatigue builds, and legs that burn early are a warning; read legs burning when skiing if that is your problem.

A good first-day target for most people is four to five hours on snow with breaks, not seven. If you feel strong, go further on day two, not on day one.

Signs to stop

  • Your thighs shake when you stand on the flats.
  • You catch your edge or lose balance on easy terrain.
  • Your hands are drifting behind your hips and you cannot fix it.
  • Any sharp pain in a knee, hip or back.
  • Dizziness, chest pain, unusual breathlessness. Stop and get help.

Having one more run is the classic mistake. If you are wondering whether to finish, you probably should.

Common first-day mistakes

  • Skiing until the lifts close. The last run of a long first day is the one people regret.
  • Following faster friends. Ski your own pace and meet at the bottom.
  • Ignoring boots. Pain in the shin or foot on day one gets worse by day three. Loosen buckles on lifts and ask a boot fitter about hot spots.
  • Skipping lunch. Low energy and fatigue in the afternoon make mistakes more likely.
  • Going straight to a big mountain day. If you can, pick a smaller resort or a half day first.

After the first day

Expect soreness the next morning and especially on day two. Walk, drink, eat and sleep as normal. Keep day two lighter than you want to. More on this in sore after skiing. If you are on a trip, plan on shorter mornings for the first two days.

If you have an old knee injury or the knee swells, hurts on stairs or feels unstable, see a physiotherapist or doctor before you ski more. The sooner you check, the more of the season you keep.

Write it down

It sounds fussy, but it works: the details fade by next autumn, and notes beat memory.

Note how you felt: which run your legs went, how long the day was, what you ate. Next season you will use it to plan your first day better. The ski day log is a good place for that.

Questions

How long should I ski on my first day of the season?

Four to five hours with breaks is a sensible target for most people. Stop earlier if your turns get sloppy or your legs shake.

Should I ski black runs on the first day?

Start on easy terrain regardless of your level, and add difficulty only once your legs and balance feel right.

Do I need to warm up before skiing?

There are no ski-specific trials for recreational skiers, but a few minutes of marching, leg swings and squats before you click in is low-cost and sensible.

Why are my legs so sore after the first day?

Skiing involves lots of braking work, which causes soreness a day or two later, particularly after time off. It usually eases as your legs readapt over the next days.

When should I see a doctor after a ski day?

For knee swelling, instability, pain that gets worse, chest pain or unusual breathlessness. Do not ski through those.
